\section*{1D Gaussian Density Estimation}

\subsection*{Features with a Good Gaussian Fit}
The Maximum Likelihood (ML) 1D Gaussian model provides a good, accurate fit for features that exhibit symmetric, bell-shaped distributions. For these features, the empirical data closely follows the theoretical exponential decay from the mean dictated by the computed variance.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fake Class:} Features 1, 2, 3, and 4. The histograms show a clear, symmetric distribution around their respective means (e.g., Feature 1 has a mean of $0.0029$ and a variance of $0.5696$, and visually matches the theoretical curve well).
    \item \textbf{Genuine Class:} Features 1, 2, 3, and 4. These features present a strong symmetric distribution around their empirical means. For instance, Feature 1 (mean $0.0005$, variance $1.4302$) and Feature 2 (mean $-0.0085$, variance $0.5783$) are well-approximated by the Gaussian bell curve without severe skewness.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Features with an Inaccurate Gaussian Fit}
The Gaussian model is highly inaccurate for features that exhibit strong asymmetry (skewness), multiple modes, or sharp drop-offs. The strict mathematical constraints of the Gaussian model (perfect symmetry around the mean) fail to capture these irregular empirical geometries.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fake Class:} Features 5 and 6 are heavily skewed to the left. For instance, despite Feature 5 having a computed ML mean of $0.0280$ and variance of $0.6801$, the visual data has sharp right-sided peaks and long left tails that the symmetric curve over-estimates or misses entirely.
    \item \textbf{Genuine Class:} Features 5 and 6 exhibit severe negative skewness. The rigid symmetry of the Gaussian model fails here because it predicts high probabilities in regions on the right side where empirical samples are virtually nonexistent, while underestimating the heavy tails on the left.
\end{itemize}
