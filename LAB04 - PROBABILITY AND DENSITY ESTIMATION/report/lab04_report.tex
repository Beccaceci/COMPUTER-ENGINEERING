\section{Gaussian Density Estimation}

\subsection*{What do you observe?}
When superimposing the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimated 1D Gaussian probability density functions over the normalized histograms, we observe that the fundamental assumption of normality (that the data follows a symmetric, bell-shaped curve) is highly inconsistent across the dataset. Because the Gaussian model forces a perfectly symmetric shape centered on the mean, it behaves predictably: it wraps nicely around symmetric data but completely fails to capture the true density of data that is heavily skewed or asymmetric.

\subsection*{Are there features for which the Gaussian densities provide a good fit?}
Yes, the Gaussian densities provide a reasonably good fit for features that naturally exhibit a roughly symmetric distribution. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fake Class:} Features 1, 2, 3, and 4 provide a solid fit. The estimated red Gaussian curves successfully capture the central density peaks and the tapering tails of the empirical blue histograms.
    \item \textbf{Genuine Class:} Features 3 and 4 show an acceptable, albeit slightly imperfect, alignment with the Gaussian fit.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{images/fake_feature_1.png}
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{images/genuine_feature_3.png}
    \caption{Examples of good Gaussian fits: Fake Feature 1 (left) and Genuine Feature 3 (right). Both exhibit a relatively symmetric, bell-shaped empirical distribution.}
    \label{fig:good_gaussian_fits}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Are there features for which the Gaussian model seems significantly less accurate?}
Absolutely. The Gaussian model is highly inaccurate for features that exhibit strong asymmetry (skewness), as the mathematical model simply cannot bend to fit a skewed shape.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fake Class:} Features 5 and 6 are heavily skewed to the left. The symmetric Gaussian curve underestimates the sharp right-sided peaks and overestimates the density on the steep drop-off.
    \item \textbf{Genuine Class:} The inaccuracy is even more pronounced here. Features 1, 2, 5, and 6 exhibit severe negative skewness (long left tails with very sharp, sudden drop-offs on the right). For instance, the model predicts a high probability of finding samples on the far right side where essentially zero empirical samples exist.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{images/fake_feature_5.png}
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\linewidth]{images/genuine_feature_5.png}
    \caption{Examples of inaccurate Gaussian fits: Fake Feature 5 (left) and Genuine Feature 5 (right). The rigid symmetry of the Gaussian model completely misses the sharp peaks and asymmetric tails.}
    \label{fig:bad_gaussian_fits}
\end{figure}

This visual analysis indicates that while a Gaussian classifier is mathematically elegant and computationally fast, its rigid structural assumptions will likely cause classification errors when evaluating test samples based on these heavily skewed features.
