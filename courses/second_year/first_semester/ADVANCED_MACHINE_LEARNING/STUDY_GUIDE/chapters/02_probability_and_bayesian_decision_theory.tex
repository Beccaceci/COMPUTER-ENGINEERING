\chapter{Probability and decision theory}
\label{sec:probability_and_decision_theory}

Machine learning is fundamentally the science of making principled inferences and optimal decisions under uncertainty. Every real-world sensor reading is perturbed by noise, every dataset represents an incomplete sample of reality, and every prediction about future test instances carries inherent ambiguity. To build algorithms that learn from data, we must ground our reasoning in the mathematical framework of \textbf{probability theory} and \textbf{statistical decision theory}.

In this chapter, we develop the foundations of probabilistic machine learning. We begin from the axiomatic formulation of probability spaces, Cox's plausibility postulates, and de Finetti's Dutch Book theorem, progress through continuous density transformations and multivariable joint distributions, establish the core foundations of information theory (entropy, KL divergence, and cross-entropy), analyze Bayes' rule with exact geometric area models, resolve probabilistic paradoxes, and explore causal confounding topologies.

We then construct generative classifiers: exploring the complete continuum from discrete and continuous Gaussian Naïve Bayes to Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) and Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), followed by an exhaustive examination of Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) versus Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) parameter learning. Finally, we establish the foundations of statistical learning theory and decision theory: formalizing task-dependent loss functions via parametric distributions, True Risk, the Bayes Optimal Classifier under asymmetric loss matrices, the reject option, regression decision theory (conditional mean and median), the information-theoretic equivalence of MLE to KL divergence minimization, and point estimator statistics with Bessel's correction.

\section{Probability foundations}
\label{sec:foundations_of_probability}

Why are basic statistics and probability indispensable for machine learning? As established in lecture, machine learning models do not possess omniscience; they process finite empirical observations to infer unobserved truths. Probability provides the formal mathematical calculus to:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Quantify our degree of belief in competing hypotheses given partial, noisy evidence.
  \item Formulate principled classification rules that minimize expected misclassification costs.
  \item Bridge observed finite datasets to unobserved continuous population distributions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sample spaces and probability measures}
\label{sec:sample_spaces_and_events}

To formalize uncertainty mathematically, we construct a probability space as a measure-theoretic triplet $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sample Space} $\Omega$: The set of all conceivable, mutually exclusive outcomes of a physical or conceptual experiment:
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \emph{Discrete Sample Spaces}: When outcomes can be enumerated. For a single coin flip, $\Omega = \{\text{Heads}, \text{Tails}\}$; for a 6-sided die, $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$; for a standard deck of cards, $|\Omega| = 52$.
    \item \emph{Continuous Sample Spaces}: When outcomes represent uncountably infinite measurements, such as sensor voltages, physical temperatures, or spatial coordinates: $\Omega = \mathbb{R}$ or $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^d$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Event} $A \subseteq \Omega$: An event is any subset of the sample space whose occurrence we wish to evaluate. For example, rolling an even number on a die corresponds to the event subset $A = \{2, 4, 6\} \subset \Omega$.
  \item \textbf{Probability Measure} $P(A)$: A real-valued function $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0, 1]$ that assigns a scalar plausibility to event $A$. Visually, if the entire sample space $\Omega$ is represented as a geometric region with unit area ($P(\Omega) = 1$), the probability of event $A$ is the fraction of total area covered by subset $A$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Kolmogorov axioms}
\label{sec:kolmogorov_axioms}

In 1933, Andrey Kolmogorov established that any valid mathematical calculus of probability must satisfy three non-negotiable axioms:
\begin{definition}[Kolmogorov Probability Axioms~\cite{bishop2024deep}]
Let $\Omega$ be a sample space and $\mathcal{F}$ be the collection of valid events. A function $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0, 1]$ is a probability measure if and only if:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Axiom 1 (Non-Negativity)}: For every event $A \subseteq \Omega$,
  \begin{equation}
  P(A) \ge 0.
  \label{eq:kolmogorov_nonnegativity}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Axiom 2 (Total Measure / Normalization)}: The probability of the entire sample space is unity:
  \begin{equation}
  P(\Omega) = 1.
  \label{eq:kolmogorov_normalization}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Axiom 3 (Countable Additivity / $\sigma$-Additivity)}: For any sequence of pairwise mutually exclusive events $\{A_1, A_2, \dots\}$ (where $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \ne j$):
  \begin{equation}
  P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty P(A_i).
  \label{eq:kolmogorov_additivity}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

From these three minimal axioms, the foundational properties of probability calculus follow directly:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Empty Set}: $P(\emptyset) = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Complementation Rule}: Since $A \cup A^c = \Omega$ and $A \cap A^c = \emptyset$, Axioms 2 and 3 imply:
  \begin{equation}
  P(A^c) = 1 - P(A).
  \label{eq:complementation_rule}
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Monotonicity}: If event $A$ is a subset of event $B$ ($A \subseteq B$), then $P(A) \le P(B)$.
  \item \textbf{Inclusion-Exclusion Principle}: For any two events $A$ and $B$ (not necessarily disjoint):
  \begin{equation}
  P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B).
  \label{eq:inclusion_exclusion_rule}
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cox's postulates}
\label{sec:cox_postulates}

A profound foundational question in machine learning is: \emph{Why must we quantify uncertainty using Kolmogorov probability calculus rather than inventing arbitrary scoring heuristics, fuzzy logics, or ad-hoc confidence metrics?}

In 1946, physicist Richard Cox~\cite{cox1946probability,jaynes2003probability} proved that any mathematical framework for plausible reasoning that satisfies basic qualitative desiderata of human common sense is \textbf{isomorphic to standard probability theory}. Cox defined three commonsense postulates:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Real-Valued Representation}: Degrees of plausibility are represented by real scalar numbers: if hypothesis $A$ is more plausible than hypothesis $B$, and $B$ is more plausible than $C$, then $p(A) > p(B) > p(C)$.
  \item \textbf{Qualitative Correspondence}: As the plausibility of hypothesis $A$ increases given background information, the plausibility of its negation $\neg A$ must strictly decrease.
  \item \textbf{Consistency}: If a conclusion can be reasoned out in multiple mathematically valid ways, every equivalent line of reasoning must yield the identical numerical plausibility value.
\end{enumerate}
From these three qualitative postulates alone, Cox proved through functional equations that the product rule $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B)$ and the sum rule $P(A) + P(\neg A) = 1$ are mathematically inevitable. \textbf{Probability theory is not an arbitrary convention; it is the unique mathematical extension of Boolean logic to conditions of uncertainty.}

\subsection{de Finetti's Dutch Book theorem}
\label{sec:dutch_book_theorem}

While Richard Cox approached the uniqueness of probability from the perspective of qualitative logic, Italian mathematician Bruno de Finetti~\cite{definetti1937prevision,russell2020artificial} provided an independent, operational justification grounded in \textbf{decision theory and rational economics}: the celebrated \textbf{Dutch Book Theorem}.

Suppose an intelligent agent asserts subjective degrees of belief in propositions and is willing to accept bets at betting odds corresponding to those numbers. Let $p \in [0, 1]$ be the agent's stated probability for event $A$. The agent agrees to accept a bet with positive or negative stake $S$:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item The agent receives $p \cdot S$ upfront.
  \item If event $A$ occurs, the agent pays the opponent $S$.
  \item The agent's net payoff is:
  \begin{equation}
  \text{Payoff} = p \cdot S - S \cdot \mathbb{I}(A) = \begin{cases} (p - 1)S & \text{if } A \text{ occurs}, \\ p S & \text{if } A \text{ does not occur}. \end{cases}
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\begin{theorem}[de Finetti's Dutch Book Theorem~\cite{definetti1937prevision,russell2020artificial}]
If an agent's subjective probabilities violate any of the Kolmogorov probability axioms, then there exists a set of simultaneous bets (a ``Dutch book'') that guarantees the agent incurs a strictly negative net payoff regardless of the actual state of the world.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof for Violation of the Complementation / Additivity Axiom]
Suppose the agent's subjective beliefs violate the sum rule for an event $A$ and its complement $A^c$:
\begin{equation}
p(A) + p(A^c) = 1 + \epsilon, \quad \text{with } \epsilon > 0.
\end{equation}
An astute bookmaker observes these irrational odds and places two simultaneous bets with the agent:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item Bet 1 on event $A$ with stake $S_1 = 1$. The bookmaker pays the agent $p(A)$.
  \item Bet 2 on event $A^c$ with stake $S_2 = 1$. The bookmaker pays the agent $p(A^c)$.
\end{enumerate}
The total money received upfront by the agent is $p(A) + p(A^c) = 1 + \epsilon$. Now examine the two possible physical states of nature:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Case 1: Event $A$ occurs.} The agent must pay the bookmaker $S_1 = 1$ on Bet 1, while Bet 2 expires worthless. The agent's net payoff is:
  \begin{equation}
  \text{Net Profit} = (1 + \epsilon) - 1 = +\epsilon \quad (\text{so the agent loses } \epsilon \text{ relative to the bookmaker's net gain of } \epsilon).
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Case 2: Event $A^c$ occurs.} The agent must pay the bookmaker $S_2 = 1$ on Bet 2, while Bet 1 expires worthless. The agent's net payoff is again:
  \begin{equation}
  \text{Net Profit} = (1 + \epsilon) - 1 = +\epsilon.
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
In every possible state of reality, the bookmaker is guaranteed to extract exactly $\epsilon > 0$ from the agent. A symmetric Dutch book forces an unavoidable loss if $p(A) + p(A^c) < 1$. Therefore, an agent who deviates from Kolmogorov's axioms is mathematically irrational in an operational sense: their beliefs permit an opponent to systematically drain their resources with zero risk.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Random variables and transformations}
\label{sec:random_variables}

A \textbf{random variable} $X$ is a formal mathematical function that maps outcomes from the sample space to real numbers:
\begin{equation}
X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}.
\end{equation}
Random variables allow us to translate physical experimental outcomes into numerical quantities amenable to algebra and calculus:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Discrete Random Variables}: Take values from a countable set $\{x_1, x_2, \dots\}$. Their distribution is specified by a \textbf{probability mass function (PMF)} $P(X = x)$, satisfying $\sum_x P(X = x) = 1$.
  \item \textbf{Continuous Random Variables}: Take values in continuous intervals of $\mathbb{R}$. Because the probability of hitting an exact real number is zero ($P(X = x) = 0$), continuous uncertainty is specified by a \textbf{probability density function (PDF)} $p(x) \ge 0$, where probabilities correspond to integrals over intervals:
  \begin{equation}
  P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b p(x) \, dx, \quad \text{with} \quad \int_{-\infty}^\infty p(x) \, dx = 1.
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Transformation of continuous densities and the Jacobian determinant.}
In machine learning, continuous random variables are routinely transformed through deterministic non-linear mappings (such as neural network layers, coordinate changes, or normalizing flows). 

Let $x$ be a continuous random variable with density $p_x(x)$, and let $y = g(x)$ be a strictly monotonic, invertible transformation. Because probability mass must be conserved across corresponding infinitesimal intervals $[x, x + \delta x]$ and $[y, y + \delta y]$:
\begin{equation}
p_y(y) |\delta y| \approx p_x(x) |\delta x|.
\end{equation}
Taking the infinitesimal limit $\delta x \to 0$ yields the continuous \textbf{change-of-variables formula}~\cite{bishop2024deep}:
\begin{equation}
p_y(y) = p_x(x) \left| \frac{dx}{dy} \right| = p_x(g^{-1}(y)) \left| \frac{d}{dy} g^{-1}(y) \right|.
\label{eq:scalar_density_transformation}
\end{equation}
For multi-dimensional random vectors $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ mapped via an invertible vector-valued function $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the scalar derivative generalizes to the absolute value of the \textbf{Jacobian determinant}:
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}) = p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}) \left| \det \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}}{\partial \mathbf{y}} \right) \right| = p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{g}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})) \left| \det \left( \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}) \right) \right|^{-1},
\label{eq:multivariate_density_transformation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the Jacobian matrix with entries $(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}})_{ij} = \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial x_j}$.

\begin{examinsight}[Mode Non-Invariance of Continuous Densities]
A fundamental concept frequently examined in oral exams is that \textbf{the mode (maximum) of a continuous probability density is not invariant under non-linear parameter transformations!}
For a discrete random variable, if $x^*$ maximizes $P_X(x)$, then $y^* = g(x^*)$ uniquely maximizes $P_Y(y)$. 
However, for continuous densities, maximizing $p_y(y)$ is not equivalent to evaluating $g(x^*)$ because the Jacobian term $\left| \frac{dx}{dy} \right|$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:scalar_density_transformation} varies with $y$. Differentiating $p_y(y)$ with respect to $y$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d p_y(y)}{dy} = \frac{d}{dy} \left[ p_x(x) \left| \frac{dx}{dy} \right| \right] = \frac{d p_x(x)}{dx} \left( \frac{dx}{dy} \right)^2 + p_x(x) \frac{d^2 x}{dy^2}.
\end{equation}
Setting this to zero reveals that the stationary point of $p_y(y)$ depends on the curvature $\frac{d^2 x}{dy^2}$ of the transformation. Consequently, Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) point estimates depend on the chosen coordinate parameterization!
\end{examinsight}

\subsection{Expectation, variance, and covariance}
\label{sec:moments_and_covariance}

To summarize probability distributions compactly, we compute their statistical moments:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mathematical Expectation} $\mathbb{E}[X]$: The probability-weighted average value of a random variable:
  \begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[X] = \begin{cases} \sum_x x P(X = x) & \text{(discrete)}, \\ \int_{-\infty}^\infty x p(x) \, dx & \text{(continuous)}. \end{cases}
  \end{equation}
  Expectation is a strictly linear operator: $\mathbb{E}[a X + b Y + c] = a \mathbb{E}[X] + b \mathbb{E}[Y] + c$ for any scalars $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$.
  \item \textbf{Variance} $\operatorname{Var}(X)$: Measures the expected squared deviation from the mean:
  \begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(X) \triangleq \mathbb{E}\left[ (X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2 \right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2.
  \end{equation}
  The standard deviation is $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$, which retains the original physical measurement units.
  \item \textbf{Covariance Matrix} $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$: For a multi-dimensional random vector $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, \dots, x_d]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}] \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the pairwise interactions are captured by the $(d \times d)$ covariance matrix:
  \begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \triangleq \mathbb{E}\left[ (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}.
  \label{eq:covariance_matrix_definition}
  \end{equation}
  The diagonal entry $\Sigma_{jj} = \operatorname{Var}(x_j)$ is the variance of feature $j$, while the off-diagonal entry $\Sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(x_i, x_j) = \mathbb{E}[(x_i - \mu_i)(x_j - \mu_j)]$ measures the linear co-variation between features $i$ and $j$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Properties of the Covariance Matrix.}
The covariance matrix satisfies two non-negotiable algebraic properties:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Symmetry}: $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^\top$, because $\operatorname{Cov}(x_i, x_j) = \operatorname{Cov}(x_j, x_i)$.
  \item \textbf{Positive Semi-Definiteness}: For any arbitrary non-zero real vector $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
  \begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}^\top \mathbb{E}\left[ (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \right] \mathbf{z} = \mathbb{E}\left[ \mathbf{z}^\top (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \mathbf{z} \right] = \mathbb{E}\left[ (\mathbf{z}^\top (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}))^2 \right] \ge 0.
  \label{eq:psd_covariance_proof}
  \end{equation}
  Because it is the expected value of a squared scalar quantity, $\mathbf{z}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{z} \ge 0$. Hence, all eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ are non-negative ($\lambda_i \ge 0$).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Independence versus Zero Covariance.}
In oral exams, students must clearly articulate that while statistical independence implies zero covariance, the converse is \textbf{strictly false}:
\begin{equation}
X \perp Y \implies \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 \centernot\implies X \perp Y.
\end{equation}
To prove this counterexample, let $X \sim \mathcal{U}[-1, 1]$ be uniformly distributed on the interval $[-1, 1]$, and let $Y = X^2$. Here, $Y$ is completely deterministic given $X$ (they are maximally dependent). However, the covariance evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[X Y] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - (0)\mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_{-1}^1 \frac{x^3}{2} \, dx = 0.
\end{equation}
The covariance is identically zero because covariance measures only \emph{linear} relationships. Non-linear dependencies escape detection by covariance alone!

\paragraph{Notation distinction in AML literature.}
Throughout this text, we adhere strictly to canonical probabilistic notation:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Capital letter $X$ denotes the \textbf{random variable} (the abstract mapping).
  \item Lowercase letter $x$ denotes a \textbf{specific scalar value} realization ($X = x$).
  \item Bold lowercase $\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denotes a multi-dimensional \textbf{feature vector}.
  \item $P(X = x)$ denotes the probability of the event that random variable $X$ takes value $x$, frequently abbreviated as $P(x)$ when the variable context is unambiguous.
\end{itemize}

\section{Multivariable distributions and inference}
\label{sec:multivariable_inference}

Real-world machine learning systems rarely observe a single isolated random variable; they observe dozens, hundreds, or millions of interacting features simultaneously.

\subsection{Joint distributions}
\label{sec:joint_distributions}

When two or more random variables interact, the probability of one variable taking a specific value depends on the values assumed by the others. We define a \textbf{joint ensemble} $(X, Y)$ governed by the \textbf{joint probability distribution}:
\begin{equation}
P(X = x, Y = y) \quad \text{or} \quad P(X = x \cap Y = y).
\end{equation}
The joint distribution provides a complete, exhaustive description of uncertainty across all variables. The sum across all possible combinations of assignments must equal unity:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(X = x, Y = y) = 1.
\label{eq:joint_normalization}
\end{equation}

Consider a discrete environmental domain with two binary variables: Temperature $T \in \{\text{hot}, \text{cold}\}$ and Weather $W \in \{\text{sun}, \text{rain}\}$. Their interaction is completely defined by a $2 \times 2$ joint distribution table, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:cs188_joint_simple}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Joint probability distribution $P(T, W)$ for Temperature and Weather.}
\label{tab:cs188_joint_simple}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
& \textbf{Weather $W = \text{sun}$} & \textbf{Weather $W = \text{rain}$} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Temperature $T = \text{hot}$} & $0.40$ & $0.10$ \\
\textbf{Temperature $T = \text{cold}$} & $0.10$ & $0.40$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

From this joint distribution table, the probability of \textbf{any} compound event can be evaluated by summing the probabilities of the atomic outcomes satisfying the event condition:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Probability that it is hot AND sunny}: Directly looked up from the table intersection:
  \begin{equation}
  P(T = \text{hot}, W = \text{sun}) = 0.40.
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Probability that it is hot OR sunny}: Evaluated via the inclusion-exclusion principle or by summing the three disjoint matching cells:
  \begin{align}
  P(T = \text{hot} \cup W = \text{sun}) &= P(T=\text{hot}, W=\text{sun}) + P(T=\text{hot}, W=\text{rain}) + P(T=\text{cold}, W=\text{sun}) \nonumber \\
  &= 0.40 + 0.10 + 0.10 = 0.60.
  \end{align}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Marginalization}
\label{sec:marginal_distributions}

In many practical scenarios, we possess a high-dimensional joint distribution over multiple variables, but we are interested in the probability distribution over a smaller subset of variables, ignoring the others. 

A \textbf{marginal distribution} is a sub-table obtained by eliminating (collapsing) irrelevant variables through summation. This fundamental operation is known as \textbf{marginalization} or \textbf{"summing out"}:
\begin{equation}
P(X = x) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(X = x, Y = y).
\label{eq:marginalization_discrete}
\end{equation}
For continuous distributions, summation is replaced by integration over the domain of the omitted variable:
\begin{equation}
p(x) = \int_{\mathcal{Y}} p(x, y) \, dy.
\label{eq:marginalization_continuous}
\end{equation}
Applying Equation~\eqref{eq:marginalization_discrete} to Table~\ref{tab:cs188_joint_simple}:
\begin{align}
P(T = \text{hot}) &= P(T=\text{hot}, W=\text{sun}) + P(T=\text{hot}, W=\text{rain}) = 0.40 + 0.10 = 0.50, \\
P(T = \text{cold}) &= P(T=\text{cold}, W=\text{sun}) + P(T=\text{cold}, W=\text{rain}) = 0.10 + 0.40 = 0.50.
\end{align}
Similarly, summing the columns yields the marginal distribution of Weather:
\begin{align}
P(W = \text{sun}) &= 0.40 + 0.10 = 0.50, \\
P(W = \text{rain}) &= 0.10 + 0.40 = 0.50.
\end{align}
Because weather has only two possible mutually exclusive values, observing $P(W = \text{sun}) = 0.50$ immediately allows us to express $P(W = \text{rain}) = 1 - P(W = \text{sun}) = 0.50$.

\subsection{Conditional probability}
\label{sec:conditional_probabilities}

\textbf{Conditional probability} quantifies our updated belief in an event $X = x$ given that we have observed deterministic evidence that another event $Y = y$ has occurred. Mathematically, conditional probability is defined as the fraction of worlds in which $X$ is true among those worlds where $Y$ is true:
\begin{equation}
P(X = x \mid Y = y) = \frac{P(X = x, Y = y)}{P(Y = y)} = \frac{P(X = x, Y = y)}{\sum_{x'} P(X = x', Y = y)}.
\label{eq:conditional_probability_def}
\end{equation}
The intuitive geometric meaning of Equation~\eqref{eq:conditional_probability_def} is \textbf{sample space contraction}: observing evidence $Y = y$ collapses our universe of possibilities from the entire sample space $\Omega$ down to the conditioning slice $Y = y$. The denominator $P(Y = y)$ acts as a local normalizing constant that rescales the probabilities within the slice so that their sum equals 1:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(X = x \mid Y = y) = 1.
\end{equation}

Applying Equation~\eqref{eq:conditional_probability_def} to our weather joint table:
\begin{align}
P(W = \text{sun} \mid T = \text{hot}) &= \frac{P(W=\text{sun}, T=\text{hot})}{P(T=\text{hot})} = \frac{0.40}{0.50} = 0.80, \\
P(W = \text{rain} \mid T = \text{hot}) &= \frac{P(W=\text{rain}, T=\text{hot})}{P(T=\text{hot})} = \frac{0.10}{0.50} = 0.20.
\end{align}
Notice how our belief in sunshine increased from its prior marginal probability $P(W = \text{sun}) = 0.50$ to $0.80$ once we observed the evidence that temperature is hot.

\subsection{Probabilistic belief updating}
\label{sec:belief_updating}

\textbf{Probabilistic inference} is the computational process of calculating a desired probability distribution from a known model and observed evidence. In machine learning, an intelligent agent maintains beliefs about unobserved state variables (e.g., traffic conditions, disease status, or semantic object labels) and dynamically updates these beliefs as new sensor measurements arrive:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Baseline belief: $P(\text{On Time} \mid \text{No Reported Accidents}) = 0.90$.
  \item Incorporating time of day: $P(\text{On Time} \mid \text{No Accidents}, 5\text{ a.m.}) = 0.95$.
  \item Incorporating weather: $P(\text{On Time} \mid \text{No Accidents}, 5\text{ a.m.}, \text{Heavy Rain}) = 0.80$.
\end{itemize}
As additional evidence is observed, the conditioning set expands, and beliefs are continuously revised according to the laws of probability.

\subsection{Inference by enumeration}
\label{sec:inference_by_enumeration}

When we possess the full joint distribution table across all variables in a domain, any probabilistic query can be answered using the general algorithm of \textbf{Inference by Enumeration} (pioneered in the UC Berkeley CS188 artificial intelligence curriculum~\cite{russell2020artificial}).

\paragraph{Inference query 1: Unconditional marginal $P(W)$.}
To compute the marginal distribution of Weather $P(W)$, we sum out the unobserved hidden variables (Season $S$ and Temperature $T$):
\begin{equation}
P(W = \text{sun}) = \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} P(S = s, T = t, W = \text{sun}).
\end{equation}
Summing the eight corresponding rows from Table~\ref{tab:cs188_joint_full}:
\begin{align}
P(W = \text{sun}) &= 0.150 + 0.020 + 0.050 + 0.060 + 0.010 + 0.085 + 0.100 + 0.120 = 0.595, \\
P(W = \text{rain}) &= 1 - P(W = \text{sun}) = 1 - 0.595 = 0.405.
\end{align}

\paragraph{Inference query 2: Conditional distribution with evidence $P(W \mid S = \text{winter}, T = \text{hot})$.}
Now suppose we observe two pieces of evidence: the season is winter, and the temperature is hot. We wish to infer the distribution of Weather $W$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Step 1: Select matching rows}. Filter Table~\ref{tab:cs188_joint_full} for rows where $S = \text{winter}$ and $T = \text{hot}$:
  \begin{align}
  P(W = \text{sun}, S = \text{winter}, T = \text{hot}) &= 0.010, \\
  P(W = \text{rain}, S = \text{winter}, T = \text{hot}) &= 0.005.
  \end{align}
  \item \textbf{Step 2: Compute marginal probability of the evidence (normalizer)}. Sum the filtered rows:
  \begin{equation}
  P(S = \text{winter}, T = \text{hot}) = 0.010 + 0.005 = 0.015.
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Step 3: Normalize to obtain posterior probabilities}. Define the normalization factor $\alpha = \frac{1}{0.015} \approx 66.67$:
  \begin{align}
  P(W = \text{sun} \mid \text{winter}, \text{hot}) &= \frac{0.010}{0.015} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667, \\
  P(W = \text{rain} \mid \text{winter}, \text{hot}) &= \frac{0.005}{0.015} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.333.
  \end{align}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Inference query 3: Conditional distribution with hidden variable $P(W \mid S = \text{winter})$.}
Suppose we observe only that $S = \text{winter}$, while Temperature $T$ is unobserved (hidden):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Step 1: Select matching rows and marginalize out hidden variable $T$}:
  \begin{align}
  P(W = \text{sun}, \text{winter}) &= P(\text{winter}, \text{hot}, \text{sun}) + P(\text{winter}, \text{cold}, \text{sun}) = 0.010 + 0.085 = 0.095, \\
  P(W = \text{rain}, \text{winter}) &= P(\text{winter}, \text{hot}, \text{rain}) + P(\text{winter}, \text{cold}, \text{rain}) = 0.005 + 0.150 = 0.155.
  \end{align}
  \item \textbf{Step 2: Compute total probability of winter}:
  \begin{equation}
  P(\text{winter}) = 0.095 + 0.155 = 0.250.
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Step 3: Normalize}:
  \begin{align}
  P(W = \text{sun} \mid \text{winter}) &= \frac{0.095}{0.250} = 0.380, \\
  P(W = \text{rain} \mid \text{winter}) &= \frac{0.155}{0.250} = 0.620.
  \end{align}
\end{enumerate}
Inference by enumeration is exact and universal. However, if a problem has $d$ discrete variables each taking $v$ values, the joint table has size $v^d$, making inference by enumeration computationally intractable ($O(v^d)$ time and space) for large-scale machine learning problems.

\subsection{Product and chain rules}
\label{sec:product_and_chain_rules}

Rearranging the definition of conditional probability in Equation~\eqref{eq:conditional_probability_def} yields the \textbf{Product Rule}:
\begin{equation}
P(X, Y) = P(X \mid Y) P(Y) = P(Y \mid X) P(X).
\label{eq:product_rule_basic}
\end{equation}
By repeatedly applying the product rule to an ensemble of $d$ random variables $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_d$, we obtain the fundamental \textbf{Chain Rule of Probability}:
\begin{align}
P(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_d) &= P(X_1) P(X_2 \mid X_1) P(X_3 \mid X_1, X_2) \dots P(X_d \mid X_1, \dots, X_{d-1}) \nonumber \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^d P(X_i \mid X_1, \dots, X_{i-1}).
\label{eq:chain_rule_general}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:chain_rule_general} is an exact identity that holds for \textbf{any} joint probability distribution without requiring any independence assumptions. It proves that any joint distribution can be decomposed into an incremental product of conditional distributions.

\subsection{Information-theoretic foundations}
\label{sec:information_theory_foundations}

In modern statistical machine learning and deep learning, quantifying information content, uncertainty, and divergence between probability distributions is essential~\cite{goodfellow2016deep,bishop2024deep}. Information theory, founded by Claude Shannon in 1948~\cite{shannon1948mathematical}, provides the formal mathematical language for this quantification.

\paragraph{Shannon Self-Information.}
How much information is conveyed when an event $x$ occurs? Shannon observed that the information content of an event should be inversely related to its probability:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Highly probable events (e.g., ``the sun rose this morning'') convey virtually zero surprise or information.
  \item Extremely rare events (e.g., ``a severe magnitude 8 earthquake just struck'') carry immense surprise and high information content.
\end{itemize}
Furthermore, if two independent events $A$ and $B$ occur simultaneously ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$), their total information content must be strictly additive: $I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B)$. The unique continuous mathematical function satisfying $f(x \cdot y) = f(x) + f(y)$ is the logarithm. Hence, the \textbf{Shannon self-information} of an event $x$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
I(x) \triangleq -\log_2 P(x) = \log_2 \frac{1}{P(x)}.
\label{eq:self_information}
\end{equation}
When base-2 logarithm is used, self-information is measured in \textbf{bits} (or \emph{shannons}). When natural logarithm $\ln$ is used, it is measured in \textbf{nats}.

\paragraph{Shannon Entropy.}
The \textbf{Shannon Entropy} $H(X)$ measures the expected information content, or average uncertainty, inherent in a random variable $X$:
\begin{equation}
H(X) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[I(x)] = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \log_2 P(x),
\label{eq:shannon_entropy_def}
\end{equation}
with the continuous limit convention that $0 \log_2 0 = \lim_{p \to 0^+} p \log_2 p = 0$. Shannon's Source Coding Theorem establishes that $H(X)$ is the fundamental mathematical lower bound on the average number of bits required to losslessly compress and transmit symbols drawn from distribution $P$.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item For a deterministic variable ($P(x_1) = 1$): $H(X) = 0$ bits (zero uncertainty).
  \item For a uniform distribution over $K$ outcomes ($P(x_k) = \frac{1}{K}$): $H(X) = \log_2 K$ bits, achieving maximal possible entropy.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence and Relative Entropy.}
Suppose a physical process generates data according to a true target distribution $P(x)$, but we approximate or model it using an empirical distribution $Q(x)$. How much extra information (or coding inefficiency) is introduced by using $Q$ instead of $P$?

This statistical distance is quantified by the \textbf{Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence} (or \textbf{Relative Entropy})~\cite{kullback1951information}:
\begin{equation}
D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \triangleq \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[ \log P(x) - \log Q(x) \right].
\label{eq:kl_divergence_def}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Gibbs' Inequality: Non-Negativity of KL Divergence]
For any two discrete probability distributions $P$ and $Q$ on $\mathcal{X}$:
\begin{equation}
D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \ge 0,
\label{eq:gibbs_inequality}
\end{equation}
with equality $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) = 0$ if and only if $P(x) = Q(x)$ for all $x \in \mathcal{X}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof via Jensen's Inequality]
Recall Jensen's inequality: for any strictly convex function $f$ and random variable $Z$, $\mathbb{E}[f(Z)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[Z])$. Because $f(t) = -\ln t$ is strictly convex on $(0, \infty)$, we write:
\begin{align}
D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) &= \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \left( -\ln \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \right) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[ -\ln \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \right] \nonumber \\
&\ge -\ln \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[ \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \right] \right) = -\ln \left( \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \right) \nonumber \\
&= -\ln \left( \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} Q(x) \right) = -\ln(1) = 0.
\end{align}
Because $-\ln t$ is strictly convex, equality holds if and only if the ratio $\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)}$ is constant almost everywhere. Since $\sum_x P(x) = \sum_x Q(x) = 1$, the constant must be $1$, proving $P(x) = Q(x)$ for all $x$.
\end{proof}

\begin{examinsight}[KL Divergence is Not a True Metric]
In oral exams, professors frequently test whether students understand why KL divergence is \textbf{not a mathematical distance metric}:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Asymmetry}: $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \ne D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P)$ in general. Optimizing $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q)$ forces $Q$ to cover all modes of $P$ (zero-avoiding / mean-seeking), whereas optimizing $D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P)$ forces $Q$ to fit inside a single mode of $P$ (zero-forcing / mode-seeking).
  \item \textbf{Triangle Inequality Failure}: KL divergence does not satisfy $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel R) \le D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) + D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel R)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{examinsight}

\paragraph{Cross-Entropy and Its Decomposition.}
The \textbf{Cross-Entropy} between target distribution $P$ and model distribution $Q$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
H(P, Q) \triangleq -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} P(x) \log Q(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[-\log Q(x)].
\label{eq:cross_entropy_def_infotheory}
\end{equation}
Expanding Equation~\eqref{eq:kl_divergence_def} yields the canonical decomposition:
\begin{align}
D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) &= \sum_{x} P(x) \log P(x) - \sum_{x} P(x) \log Q(x) = -H(P) + H(P, Q) \nonumber \\
\implies H(P, Q) &= H(P) + D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q).
\label{eq:cross_entropy_kl_decomposition}
\end{align}
When training neural networks with empirical data distribution $P = \tilde{p}_{\text{data}}$, the target data entropy $H(P)$ is a constant independent of network weights $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Therefore, \textbf{minimizing cross-entropy loss is mathematically identical to minimizing the KL divergence $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q)$ from the empirical data to the model!}

\paragraph{Joint Entropy, Conditional Entropy, and Mutual Information.}
For a joint ensemble $(X, Y)$, we define:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Joint Entropy}: $H(X, Y) \triangleq -\sum_{x, y} P(x, y) \log_2 P(x, y)$.
  \item \textbf{Conditional Entropy}: $H(Y \mid X) \triangleq -\sum_{x, y} P(x, y) \log_2 P(y \mid x) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}[H(Y \mid X = x)]$.
  \item \textbf{Mutual Information}: Quantifies the reduction in uncertainty of $X$ due to observing $Y$:
  \begin{equation}
  I(X; Y) \triangleq H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = D_{\text{KL}}(P(X, Y) \parallel P(X)P(Y)).
  \label{eq:mutual_information_def}
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\section{Bayes' rule and independence}
\label{sec:bayes_rule_and_paradoxes}

\subsection{Derivation of Bayes' rule}
\label{sec:bayes_rule_derivation}

Equating the two symmetric formulations of the product rule from Equation~\eqref{eq:product_rule_basic}:
\begin{equation}
P(Y \mid X) P(X) = P(X \mid Y) P(Y),
\end{equation}
and dividing both sides by $P(X)$ produces \textbf{Bayes' Rule}:
\begin{equation}
P(Y \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid Y) P(Y)}{P(X)}.
\label{eq:bayes_rule_classic}
\end{equation}
Applying the law of total probability to expand the evidence denominator $P(X)$ over all mutually exclusive hypotheses $\{y_1, y_2, \dots, y_K\} \in \mathcal{Y}$:
\begin{equation}
P(Y = y_k \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid Y = y_k) P(Y = y_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^K P(X \mid Y = y_j) P(Y = y_j)}.
\label{eq:bayes_rule_expanded}
\end{equation}

The four constituents of Bayes' rule define the epistemological vocabulary of statistical inference:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Prior Probability} $P(Y)$: The baseline degree of belief in hypothesis $Y$ \textbf{before} observing feature evidence $X$.
  \item \textbf{Likelihood} $P(X \mid Y)$: The generative probability that hypothesis $Y$ would produce the observed evidence $X$.
  \item \textbf{Evidence (Marginal Likelihood)} $P(X)$: The total probability of observing evidence $X$, computed by summing the joint probability across all possible hypotheses. It acts as the normalizing constant ensuring the posterior distribution sums to 1.
  \item \textbf{Posterior Probability} $P(Y \mid X)$: The updated, rational degree of belief in hypothesis $Y$ \textbf{after} incorporating evidence $X$.
\end{enumerate}

Even if the likelihood $P(E \mid H)$ is high (tall bar), if the prior $P(H)$ is minuscule (very narrow column), the right shaded area (false positives) can easily dwarf the left shaded area, explaining why rare disease screenings often produce low posterior true-positive rates.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Monty Hall problem}
\label{sec:monty_hall_problem}

To demonstrate how counter-intuitive probabilistic reasoning can be when intuition conflicts with rigorous Bayes conditioning, consider the famous \textbf{Monty Hall Problem} (dramatized in the movie \emph{21}, where MIT blackjack students exploit basic statistics to win in Las Vegas).

\paragraph{Problem setup.}
A game show contestant is presented with three closed doors: $A$, $B$, and $C$.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Behind one door is a valuable sports car (the prize).
  \item Behind the other two doors are goats (no prize).
\end{itemize}
The game proceeds in three stages:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item The contestant chooses a door (without loss of generality, let the contestant choose \textbf{Door A}).
  \item The host (Monty Hall), who knows what is behind every door, opens one of the two remaining doors (Door $B$ or Door $C$) to reveal a goat. Suppose the host opens \textbf{Door C}, showing a goat.
  \item The host then offers the contestant a choice: \emph{"Do you want to stick with your original Door A, or do you want to switch to Door B?"}
\end{enumerate}
Should the contestant stick, switch, or does it make no difference? Most people assume that since two closed doors remain, the probability is $50/50$. This intuition is mathematically false: \textbf{switching doubles your probability of winning from $1/3$ to $2/3$!}

\paragraph{Rigorous Bayesian derivation.}
Let random variable $C_{\text{prize}} \in \{A, B, C\}$ denote the door hiding the car. The prior probabilities are uniform:
\begin{equation}
P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = \frac{1}{3}.
\end{equation}
Suppose the contestant chooses Door $A$. Let the observed evidence event be $H_C$: \emph{"The host opens Door C"}.

We now compute the likelihood $P(H_C \mid C_{\text{prize}})$ under each possible location of the car:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item If the car is behind \textbf{Door A} ($C_{\text{prize}} = A$): The host can open either Door $B$ or Door $C$ without revealing the car. Assuming the host picks randomly between them:
  \begin{equation}
  P(H_C \mid A) = \frac{1}{2}.
  \end{equation}
  \item If the car is behind \textbf{Door B} ($C_{\text{prize}} = B$): The host cannot open Door $A$ (chosen by contestant) and cannot open Door $B$ (car is there). The host is \textbf{forced} to open Door $C$:
  \begin{equation}
  P(H_C \mid B) = 1.
  \end{equation}
  \item If the car is behind \textbf{Door C} ($C_{\text{prize}} = C$): The host cannot open Door $C$ because the car is behind it:
  \begin{equation}
  P(H_C \mid C) = 0.
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

Now, compute the total marginal probability of evidence $H_C$ using the law of total probability:
\begin{align}
P(H_C) &= P(H_C \mid A)P(A) + P(H_C \mid B)P(B) + P(H_C \mid C)P(C) \nonumber \\
&= \left(\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3}\right) + \left(1 \times \frac{1}{3}\right) + \left(0 \times \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}.
\end{align}

Finally, apply Bayes' rule to compute the posterior probabilities:
\begin{align}
P(\text{Stick}) &= P(A \mid H_C) = \frac{P(H_C \mid A)P(A)}{P(H_C)} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3}}{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{3}, \\
P(\text{Switch}) &= P(B \mid H_C) = \frac{P(H_C \mid B)P(B)}{P(H_C)} = \frac{1 \times \frac{1}{3}}{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{2}{3}.
\end{align}
Because $P(B \mid H_C) = \frac{2}{3} = 2 \times P(A \mid H_C)$, the optimal rational strategy is \textbf{always to switch}. The host's action transfers all the unallocated probability mass from the unchosen doors into Door $B$.

\subsection{Conditional independence and confounding}
\label{sec:independence_and_confounding}

\paragraph{Unconditional statistical independence.}
Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are \textbf{statistically independent} ($X \perp Y$) if and only if their joint distribution factorizes into the product of their marginals:
\begin{equation}
P(X, Y) = P(X) P(Y) \iff P(X \mid Y) = P(X).
\label{eq:unconditional_independence}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \emph{Independent Example}: Winning the national lottery this week versus winning it next week. The two draws are physically uncorrelated; knowing the result of one provides zero information about the other.
  \item \emph{Dependent Example}: Russian roulette. In a revolver cylinder with 6 chambers and 1 bullet, the probability of firing a blank on trigger pull 1 is $5/6$. If you survive pull 1, the probability of firing the bullet on pull 2 increases to $1/5$, because the sample space of remaining chambers contracted from 6 to 5 without spinning. The trials are statistically dependent.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Conditional independence.}
Two random variables $X$ and $Y$ are \textbf{conditionally independent} given a third variable $Z$ ($X \perp Y \mid Z$) if and only if:
\begin{equation}
P(X, Y \mid Z) = P(X \mid Z) P(Y \mid Z) \iff P(X \mid Y, Z) = P(X \mid Z).
\label{eq:conditional_independence_def}
\end{equation}
Once $Z$ is known, learning $Y$ provides no additional information about $X$.

\paragraph{The Russell \& Norvig dental clinic domain.}
Consider three variables: Toothache ($T$), Cavity ($C$), and the dentist's metal probe Catch ($M$)~\cite{russell2020artificial}. Toothache and Catch are strongly correlated: patients with toothaches are much more likely to have the probe catch on a tooth. 

However, they are \textbf{conditionally independent given Cavity}:
\begin{equation}
P(T, M \mid C) = P(T \mid C) P(M \mid C).
\end{equation}
If the dentist directly inspects the tooth and confirms a cavity is present ($C = \text{true}$), whether the probe catches depends strictly on the physical shape of the cavity, not on whether the patient feels pain. The latent cause $C$ completely explains the correlation between the two symptoms.

\paragraph{The London taxi driver confounding mystery.}
The societal danger of ignoring conditional independence is illustrated by an empirical study in London:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Observed Correlation}: Traffic safety analysts observed a statistically significant positive correlation between London taxi drivers wearing heavy winter coats ($C$) and the occurrence of vehicle traffic accidents ($A$): $P(A \mid C) > P(A \mid \neg C)$.
  \item \textbf{Naive Policy Reaction}: Transport regulators considered a municipal decree banning taxi drivers from wearing coats while driving, hypothesizing that coats restricted arm mobility and caused accidents!
  \item \textbf{The Confounding Reality}: The true underlying causal variable was \textbf{Rain / Sleet Weather} ($R$). On rainy days, roads become slick and braking distances double (causing accidents), while temperatures drop (causing drivers to wear coats).
\end{itemize}
Wearing a coat and having an accident are \textbf{conditionally independent given rain}:
\begin{equation}
P(A, C \mid R) = P(A \mid R) P(C \mid R).
\end{equation}
Conditioning on the true confounding variable $R$ dissolves the spurious correlation, proving that banning coats would have had zero effect on road safety.

\paragraph{Graphical Causal Topologies and d-Separation.}
In probabilistic graphical models and causal machine learning~\cite{russell2020artificial,bishop2024deep}, conditional independence relationships are formalized through three fundamental network motifs:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Fork (Common Cause / Confounder)}: $X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y$. 
  Variable $Z$ is a common cause of both $X$ and $Y$. In the absence of knowledge about $Z$, the path is open: $X$ and $Y$ are correlated ($X \not\perp Y$). However, conditioning on the common cause $Z$ \textbf{blocks the causal path}, rendering $X$ and $Y$ conditionally independent ($X \perp Y \mid Z$). This directly formalizes the London taxi driver phenomenon (where $Z$ is rainy weather).
  \item \textbf{The Chain (Causal Mediation)}: $X \rightarrow Z \rightarrow Y$.
  Variable $X$ causally influences $Z$, which in turn influences $Y$. The marginal path is open ($X \not\perp Y$). However, once intermediate mediator $Z$ is fully observed, all information transmitted from $X$ to $Y$ is mediated through $Z$. Hence, conditioning on $Z$ blocks the path: $X \perp Y \mid Z$.
  \item \textbf{The Collider (V-structure / Berkson's Paradox)}: $X \rightarrow Z \leftarrow Y$.
  Both $X$ and $Y$ independently influence common effect $Z$. Marginally, $X$ and $Y$ are completely independent ($X \perp Y$). However, \textbf{conditioning on the collider $Z$ opens the path}, creating an artificial dependence between $X$ and $Y$ ($X \not\perp Y \mid Z$). This counter-intuitive phenomenon is known as \textbf{explaining away} or \emph{Berkson's paradox}: observing that a student was accepted into an elite university ($Z=1$) given that their grades are low ($X=0$) heavily implies their athletic skill must be extraordinarily high ($Y=1$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Naïve Bayes and estimation}
\label{sec:naive_bayes_classifier}

We now construct our first complete, formal probabilistic machine learning classifier: the \textbf{Naïve Bayes Classifier}.

\subsection{Model-based classification}
\label{sec:naive_bayes_formulation}

Let each training instance be represented by a feature vector of $d$ attributes:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x} = [x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d]^\top \in \mathcal{X},
\end{equation}
and let the target label be a discrete categorical variable $y \in \mathcal{Y} = \{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_K\}$.

Given an observed feature vector $\mathbf{x}$, the optimal probabilistic decision assigns $\mathbf{x}$ to the class that maximizes the posterior probability (the Maximum A Posteriori, or MAP, decision rule):
\begin{equation}
\hat{y} = \argmax_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \argmax_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \frac{P(\mathbf{x} \mid y) P(y)}{P(\mathbf{x})}.
\label{eq:map_decision_rule}
\end{equation}
Because the evidence denominator $P(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_y P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)P(y)$ is identical for all candidate classes $y$, it does not alter the $\argmax$ ranking. We can safely discard it:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y} = \argmax_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(\mathbf{x} \mid y) P(y).
\label{eq:unnormalized_map_rule}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Naïve Bayes assumption}
\label{sec:naive_independence_assumption}

Estimating the prior $P(y)$ from training data is trivial: we simply count class frequencies $P(y = c) \approx \frac{N_c}{N}$. 

However, estimating the class-conditional joint likelihood $P(\mathbf{x} \mid y) = P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d \mid y)$ directly is computationally and statistically impossible in high dimensions:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item If all $d$ features are binary ($x_j \in \{0, 1\}$), there are $2^d$ possible feature combinations for each of the $K$ classes.
  \item To populate this joint likelihood table directly without assumptions requires estimating:
  \begin{equation}
  K \times (2^d - 1) = O(K \cdot 2^d) \quad \text{parameters!}
  \end{equation}
  For $d = 30$ features and $K = 2$ classes, $2 \times 2^{30} \approx 2.14 \times 10^9$ parameters—vastly exceeding the number of training samples in any realistic dataset.
\end{itemize}

The \textbf{Naïve Bayes assumption} resolves this exponential bottleneck by making a radical, simplifying architectural assumption: \textbf{All features $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d$ are conditionally independent given the class label $y$}:
\begin{equation}
P(\mathbf{x} \mid y) = P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d \mid y) = \prod_{j=1}^d P(x_j \mid y).
\label{eq:naive_bayes_assumption}
\end{equation}
Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:naive_bayes_assumption} into the MAP decision rule yields the \textbf{Naïve Bayes Classifier}:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{\text{NB}} = \argmax_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(y) \prod_{j=1}^d P(x_j \mid y).
\label{eq:naive_bayes_classifier_equation}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Exponential parameter reduction.}
Under the Naïve Bayes assumption:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Instead of estimating full joint distributions, we estimate only $d$ one-dimensional marginal conditionals $P(x_j \mid y)$ for each class.
  \item For binary features, each 1D distribution requires only 1 parameter, collapsing the total parameter count from exponential to linear:
  \begin{equation}
  O(K \cdot 2^d) \xrightarrow{\text{Naïve Bayes Assumption}} O(K \cdot d).
  \label{eq:naive_bayes_complexity_collapse}
  \end{equation}
  For $d = 30$ and $K = 2$, parameter complexity drops from $2 \times 10^9$ down to just $2 \times 30 = 60$ parameters!
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Phase 1: Estimating prior probabilities.}
From the $N = 14$ days, we count $N_{\text{Yes}} = 8$ and $N_{\text{No}} = 6$:
\begin{equation}
P(y = \text{Yes}) = \frac{8}{14} \approx 0.571, \quad P(y = \text{No}) = \frac{6}{14} \approx 0.429.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Phase 2: Estimating class-conditional likelihoods.}
We count attribute occurrences within each class subset:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Sky attribute:
  \begin{align}
  P(\text{Sky}=\text{Sunny} \mid \text{Yes}) &= 3/8, \quad P(\text{Sky}=\text{Sunny} \mid \text{No}) = 2/6, \\

  P(\text{Sky}=\text{Rainy} \mid \text{Yes}) &= 1/8, \quad P(\text{Sky}=\text{Rainy} \mid \text{No}) = 4/6.
  \end{align}
  \item Wind attribute:
  \begin{align}

\paragraph{Phase 3: Classifying a novel test instance.}
Suppose a new weekend arrives with conditions:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}^* = [\text{Sky}=\text{Sunny}, \text{AirTemp}=\text{Cold}, \text{Humidity}=\text{High}, \text{Wind}=\text{Strong}, \text{Water}=\text{Warm}, \text{Forecast}=\text{Same}]^\top.
\end{equation}

\begin{align}
\text{Score}(\text{Yes}) &= P(\text{Yes}) \prod_{j=1}^6 P(x_j^* \mid \text{Yes}) = \frac{8}{14} \times \left(\frac{3}{8} \times \frac{2}{8} \times \frac{5}{8} \times \frac{7}{8} \times \frac{5}{8} \times \frac{6}{8}\right) \approx 0.00618, \\
\text{Score}(\text{No}) &= P(\text{No}) \prod_{j=1}^6 P(x_j^* \mid \text{No}) = \frac{6}{14} \times \left(\frac{2}{6} \times \frac{3}{6} \times \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{4}{6} \times \frac{3}{6}\right) \approx 0.01029.
\end{align}

\subsection{Laplace smoothing}
\label{sec:laplace_smoothing}

\paragraph{The catastrophic zero-frequency flaw.}
Consider what happens if we attempt to classify a test day where $\text{Sky} = \text{Overcast}$ under the class $y = \text{No}$. Looking at Table~\ref{tab:enjoysport_dataset}, an overcast sky was never observed among the days where $y = \text{No}$. Thus, the maximum likelihood estimate evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
P(\text{Sky} = \text{Overcast} \mid y = \text{No}) = \frac{0}{6} = 0.
\end{equation}
Because the Naïve Bayes score is a multiplicative product across all attributes, a single zero probability completely destroys all other evidence:
\begin{equation}
\text{Score}(\text{No}) = P(\text{No}) \times 0 \times \prod_{j=2}^d P(x_j^* \mid \text{No}) \equiv 0!
\end{equation}
Even if all five other attributes overwhelmingly indicate $y = \text{No}$, the model is mathematically prohibited from predicting No, solely because a specific attribute value was missing in the finite training sample.

\paragraph{The mathematical fix: Laplace (Add-1) smoothing.}
To eliminate the zero-frequency catastrophe, we apply \textbf{Laplace Smoothing} (also known as Add-1 smoothing). We add a pseudo-count of 1 to every possible attribute outcome, adjusting the denominator by the cardinality $|V_j|$ (the number of distinct discrete values attribute $j$ can assume):
\begin{equation}
\hat{P}_{\text{Laplace}}(x_j = v \mid y = c) = \frac{\operatorname{count}(x_j = v, y = c) + 1}{\operatorname{count}(y = c) + |V_j|}.
\label{eq:laplace_smoothing_formula}
\end{equation}
Notice that the probabilities remain strictly normalized:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{v \in V_j} \hat{P}_{\text{Laplace}}(x_j = v \mid y = c) = \frac{\sum_v \operatorname{count}(x_j = v, y = c) + |V_j|}{\operatorname{count}(y = c) + |V_j|} = \frac{\operatorname{count}(y = c) + |V_j|}{\operatorname{count}(y = c) + |V_j|} = 1.
\end{equation}

For the overcast attribute ($|V_{\text{Sky}}| = 3$ for Sunny, Overcast, Rainy):
\begin{equation}
\hat{P}_{\text{Laplace}}(\text{Sky}=\text{Overcast} \mid \text{No}) = \frac{0 + 1}{6 + 3} = \frac{1}{9} > 0.
\end{equation}
The probability is no longer zero, preserving the influence of all other feature evidence. 

A generalized version is \textbf{Lidstone Smoothing} (Add-$k$ smoothing), where pseudo-count $\alpha > 0$ is a tunable hyperparameter:
\begin{equation}
\hat{P}_{\alpha}(x_j = v \mid y = c) = \frac{\operatorname{count}(x_j = v, y = c) + \alpha}{\operatorname{count}(y = c) + \alpha |V_j|}.
\label{eq:lidstone_smoothing}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Generative versus discriminative models}
\label{sec:generative_vs_discriminative}

Naïve Bayes exemplifies the \textbf{generative learning paradigm}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Generative Classifiers} (e.g., Naïve Bayes, Gaussian Mixture Models, Variational Autoencoders): Model the joint distribution:
  \begin{equation}
  P(\mathbf{x}, y) = P(y) P(\mathbf{x} \mid y).
  \end{equation}
  A generative model learns how each class generates its observed features. It can not only classify inputs, but can also generate synthetic samples $\mathbf{x} \sim P(\mathbf{x} \mid y)$.
  \item \textbf{Discriminative Classifiers} (e.g., Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines, Deep Neural Networks): Model the posterior class boundary directly:
  \begin{equation}
  P(y \mid \mathbf{x}).
  \end{equation}
  A discriminative model bypasses the difficult task of modeling the feature distribution $P(\mathbf{x})$, focusing all its parameter capacity on learning the decision boundary separating classes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{MLE and MAP estimation}
\label{sec:map_vs_mle}

When training probabilistic classifiers such as Naïve Bayes, how do we estimate the numerical parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (e.g., class priors $P(y)$ and feature probabilities $P(x_j \mid y)$) from an observed finite training dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{\mathbf{x}_i, y_i\}_{i=1}^N$?

In statistical learning, two competing philosophical and mathematical frameworks exist: \textbf{Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)} and \textbf{Maximum A Posteriori (MAP)}.

\paragraph{1. The Philosophical Foundations: Frequentist vs. Bayesian.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The Frequentist / MLE Paradigm}: 
  In the frequentist framework, the true parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$ is a \textbf{fixed, deterministic, but unknown constant of nature}. It is not a random variable, and writing a probability distribution over parameters ($p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$) is considered philosophically meaningless. Probability is interpreted strictly as the long-run empirical frequency of repeatable events. 
  
  The objective of Maximum Likelihood Estimation is to find the single parameter vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MLE}}$ that makes the observed training sample $\mathcal{D}$ most probable under the model:
  \begin{equation}
  \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MLE}} \triangleq \argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ln p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}).
  \label{eq:mle_fundamental_def}
  \end{equation}
  
  \item \textbf{The Bayesian / MAP Paradigm}:
  In the Bayesian framework, parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are treated as \textbf{random variables} endowed with epistemic uncertainty. Before observing any data, the practitioner specifies a \textbf{prior distribution} $p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ reflecting background knowledge, physical constraints, or inductive biases. 
  
  As training data $\mathcal{D}$ arrives, Bayes' rule is applied to compute the full \textbf{posterior distribution}:
  \begin{equation}
  p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{p(\mathcal{D})} = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}') p(\boldsymbol{\theta}') \, d\boldsymbol{\theta}'}.
  \label{eq:bayesian_posterior_formula}
  \end{equation}
  Because computing the marginal evidence integral in the denominator is often analytically intractable, Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) simplifies full Bayesian inference by seeking the \textbf{mode} (the single most probable point) of the posterior distribution:
  \begin{equation}
  \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MAP}} \triangleq \argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{D}) = \argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[ \ln p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \ln p(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \right].
  \label{eq:map_fundamental_def}
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{2. How and Why the Likelihood and Parameter Formulas Change.}
To understand the exact mathematical mechanism distinguishing MLE from MAP, consider a concrete binary feature (e.g., $x_j \in \{0, 1\}$ or coin flipping) governed by a Bernoulli distribution with parameter $\theta = P(x_j = 1 \mid y)$. Suppose in our training data for class $y$, we observe $N$ instances, of which $k$ have $x_j = 1$ and $N - k$ have $x_j = 0$.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The MLE Derivation}:
  Under the i.i.d. assumption, the data likelihood is:
  \begin{equation}
  L(\theta) = p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) = \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{N - k}.
  \end{equation}
  Taking the natural logarithm yields the log-likelihood:
  \begin{equation}
  \ln L(\theta) = k \ln \theta + (N - k) \ln (1 - \theta).
  \end{equation}
  To find the maximum, we compute the first derivative with respect to $\theta$ and set it to zero:
  \begin{equation}
  \frac{d \ln L(\theta)}{d\theta} = \frac{k}{\theta} - \frac{N - k}{1 - \theta} = 0 \implies k(1 - \theta) = (N - k)\theta \implies k = N\theta.
  \end{equation}
  Solving for $\theta$ yields the standard \textbf{Maximum Likelihood Estimator}:
  \begin{equation}
  \hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{k}{N}.
  \label{eq:mle_bernoulli_solution}
  \end{equation}
  Notice the severe failure mode: if an event is rare and happens not to appear in the sample ($k = 0$), $\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = 0$. This triggers the zero-frequency catastrophe in Naïve Bayes.

  \item \textbf{The MAP Derivation with a Conjugate Beta Prior}:
  In the MAP framework, we choose a conjugate prior for the Bernoulli likelihood: the \textbf{Beta distribution}:
  \begin{equation}
  p(\theta) = \operatorname{Beta}(\theta; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha, \beta)} \theta^{\alpha - 1} (1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}, \quad \alpha > 0, \; \beta > 0,
  \end{equation}
  where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are hyperparameters representing \textbf{virtual pseudocounts} (prior belief that we have previously observed $\alpha - 1$ positive outcomes and $\beta - 1$ negative outcomes before seeing the data).
  
  The posterior distribution is proportional to likelihood times prior:
  \begin{equation}
  p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \propto \left[ \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{N - k} \right] \times \left[ \theta^{\alpha - 1} (1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1} \right] = \theta^{k + \alpha - 1} (1 - \theta)^{N - k + \beta - 1}.
  \end{equation}
  Notice that the posterior is \emph{another Beta distribution}: $\operatorname{Beta}(\theta; k + \alpha, N - k + \beta)$. This closure property defines a \textbf{conjugate prior}.
  
  Taking the log-posterior objective $\mathcal{J}_{\text{MAP}}(\theta)$:
  \begin{equation}
  \mathcal{J}_{\text{MAP}}(\theta) = (k + \alpha - 1) \ln \theta + (N - k + \beta - 1) \ln (1 - \theta) + \text{constant}.
  \end{equation}
  Differentiating with respect to $\theta$ and setting to zero:
  \begin{equation}
  \frac{d \mathcal{J}_{\text{MAP}}(\theta)}{d\theta} = \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{\theta} - \frac{N - k + \beta - 1}{1 - \theta} = 0.
  \end{equation}
  Cross-multiplying:
  \begin{equation}
  (k + \alpha - 1)(1 - \theta) = (N - k + \beta - 1)\theta \implies k + \alpha - 1 = (N + \alpha + \beta - 2)\theta.
  \end{equation}
  Solving for $\theta$ yields the \textbf{Maximum A Posteriori Estimator}:
  \begin{equation}
  \hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{N + \alpha + \beta - 2}.
  \label{eq:map_bernoulli_solution}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why the Formula Changes: The Prior as an Additive Pseudocount Regularizer.}
Comparing Equation~\eqref{eq:mle_bernoulli_solution} with Equation~\eqref{eq:map_bernoulli_solution} reveals the exact mathematical origin of smoothing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If we select a uniform, completely flat prior ($\alpha = 1, \beta = 1$), the prior density is constant $p(\theta) = 1$. The MAP formula reduces exactly to the MLE formula:
  \begin{equation}
  \hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{k + 1 - 1}{N + 1 + 1 - 2} = \frac{k}{N} = \hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}.
  \end{equation}
  \item If we select Laplace's symmetric prior $\alpha = 2, \beta = 2$ (representing the assumption of having observed 1 virtual positive and 1 virtual negative sample):
  \begin{equation}
  \hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{k + 2 - 1}{N + 2 + 2 - 2} = \frac{k + 1}{N + 2}.
  \end{equation}
  \textbf{Laplace smoothing is mathematically identical to MAP estimation under a $\operatorname{Beta}(2, 2)$ prior!}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Generalization to Categorical Variables and Dirichlet Priors.}
For general multi-valued categorical features $x_j \in \{v_1, \dots, v_M\}$ with counts $N_1, \dots, N_M$ ($\sum_{m=1}^M N_m = N$), the conjugate prior to the Multinomial / Categorical likelihood is the \textbf{Dirichlet distribution} $\operatorname{Dir}(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_M; \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_M) \propto \prod_{m=1}^M \theta_m^{\alpha_m - 1}$. 

Following the identical derivation yields:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\theta}_m^{\text{MLE}} = \frac{N_m}{N}, \qquad \hat{\theta}_m^{\text{MAP}} = \frac{N_m + \alpha_m - 1}{N + \sum_{j=1}^M \alpha_j - M}.
\label{eq:dirichlet_map_formula}
\end{equation}
Setting symmetric pseudocounts $\alpha_m = 2$ strictly recovers the general Laplace estimator in Equation~\eqref{eq:laplace_smoothing_formula}:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\theta}_m^{\text{MAP}} = \frac{N_m + 1}{N + M}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Asymptotic Convergence: The Domination of Data.}
Notice what happens to the MAP estimator as the training dataset size $N \to \infty$:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{N \to \infty} \hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{N + \alpha + \beta - 2} = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{k/N + (\alpha - 1)/N}{1 + (\alpha + \beta - 2)/N} = \frac{k}{N} = \hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}}.
\end{equation}
This establishes a universal principle of statistical estimation: \textbf{as sample size grows, the empirical data likelihood $\mathcal{O}(N)$ overwhelms the static prior $\mathcal{O}(1)$, and the MAP estimate converges asymptotically to the MLE estimate.} The prior protects against variance in small samples, while allowing empirical reality to dictate parameters in large samples.

\subsection{Gaussian Naïve Bayes}
\label{sec:continuous_gaussian_nb}

When input features are continuous real numbers $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (e.g., patient blood pressure, temperatures, acoustic frequency spectra), the discrete frequency counting of Section~\ref{sec:naive_bayes_walkthrough} cannot be applied directly because the probability of any exact real number is zero ($P(X = x) = 0$).

To handle continuous attributes, \textbf{Gaussian Naïve Bayes} assumes that within each class $y = c$, each feature $x_j$ is distributed according to a one-dimensional Gaussian (Normal) density:
\begin{equation}
p(x_j \mid y = c) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma_{cj}^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x_j - \mu_{cj})^2}{2\sigma_{cj}^2} \right),
\label{eq:gaussian_nb_feature_density}
\end{equation}
parameterized by class-conditional mean $\mu_{cj}$ and variance $\sigma_{cj}^2$.

Under the Naïve Bayes conditional independence assumption, the joint class likelihood is:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c) = \prod_{j=1}^d p(x_j \mid y = c) = \prod_{j=1}^d \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma_{cj}^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x_j - \mu_{cj})^2}{2\sigma_{cj}^2} \right).
\end{equation}
Taking the natural logarithm:
\begin{equation}
\ln p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c) = -\frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^d \ln(2\pi \sigma_{cj}^2) - \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{(x_j - \mu_{cj})^2}{2\sigma_{cj}^2}.
\label{eq:gaussian_nb_log_likelihood}
\end{equation}
Given training instances $\mathcal{D}_c = \{\mathbf{x}_i : y_i = c\}$ of size $N_c$, the Maximum Likelihood parameters are computed in closed form:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mu}_{cj} = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{i: y_i = c} x_{ij}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}_{cj}^2 = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{i: y_i = c} (x_{ij} - \hat{\mu}_{cj})^2.
\end{equation}
The decision rule assigns $\mathbf{x}$ to the class maximizing $\ln P(y = c) + \ln p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c)$.

\subsection{From Naïve Bayes to LDA and QDA}
\label{sec:lda_qda_continuum}

Gaussian Naïve Bayes models each feature independently. What happens if we remove the conditional independence assumption and allow full multivariate interactions between continuous features?

Consider a generative classifier modeling each class $y = c$ via a full multivariate Gaussian distribution~\cite{bishop2024deep}:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = c) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c|^{1/2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c)^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c) \right),
\label{eq:multivariate_gaussian_class}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the class mean vector and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the class covariance matrix.

The shape and geometry of the resulting decision boundary depend entirely on the structural constraints imposed on the covariance matrices $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Gaussian Naïve Bayes (GNB)}:
  Assumes all features are conditionally independent. This constrains each $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c$ to be a strictly \textbf{diagonal matrix}:
  \begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{c1}^2, \sigma_{c2}^2, \dots, \sigma_{cd}^2).
  \end{equation}
  Geometrically, the contours of equal probability density form \textbf{axis-aligned orthogonal ellipsoids}. Feature correlations are completely ignored.

  \item \textbf{Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)}:
  Allows features to be correlated, but assumes that \textbf{all classes share an identical, pooled covariance matrix}:
  \begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c = \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \quad \text{for all } c \in \{1, \dots, K\}.
  \end{equation}
  Now consider the log-posterior odds ratio between class 1 and class 0 in binary classification:
  \begin{equation}
  a(\mathbf{x}) \triangleq \ln \frac{P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})}{P(y = 0 \mid \mathbf{x})} = \ln \frac{p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = 1) P(y = 1)}{p(\mathbf{x} \mid y = 0) P(y = 0)}.
  \end{equation}
  Substituting Equation~\eqref{eq:multivariate_gaussian_class} with shared $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$:
  \begin{align}
  a(\mathbf{x}) &= -\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1)^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1) + \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0) + \ln \frac{P(y = 1)}{P(y = 0)} \nonumber \\
  &= -\frac{1}{2}\left[ \mathbf{x}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x} - 2\boldsymbol{\mu}_1^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\mu}_1^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 \right] \nonumber \\
  &\quad + \frac{1}{2}\left[ \mathbf{x}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x} - 2\boldsymbol{\mu}_0^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\mu}_0^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 \right] + \ln \frac{P(y = 1)}{P(y = 0)}.
  \end{align}
  Notice the remarkable cancellation: \textbf{the quadratic terms $\mathbf{x}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}$ cancel out identically!}
  
  The log-odds reduces to a strictly \textbf{linear affine function of $\mathbf{x}$}:
  \begin{equation}
  a(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + w_0,
  \label{eq:lda_linear_form}
  \end{equation}
  where:
  \begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w} \triangleq \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0), \qquad w_0 \triangleq -\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_0^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 + \ln \frac{P(y=1)}{P(y=0)}.
  \label{eq:lda_weights_definition}
  \end{equation}
  Inverting the log-odds ratio $a = \ln \frac{P}{1 - P}$ yields the posterior probability:
  \begin{equation}
  P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-a(\mathbf{x})}} = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + w_0).
  \label{eq:lda_logistic_sigmoid_posterior}
  \end{equation}
  \textbf{Linear Discriminant Analysis strictly produces a planar linear decision boundary ($\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + w_0 = 0$) and directly recovers the logistic sigmoid functional form of Logistic Regression!}

  \item \textbf{Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA)}:
  If we permit each class to possess its own distinct, unconstrained covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c$, the quadratic terms $\mathbf{x}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c^{-1}\mathbf{x}$ do not cancel. 
  
  The decision boundary equation $a(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ contains quadratic forms in $\mathbf{x}$, resulting in a \textbf{quadratic hypersurface boundary} (parabolic, hyperbolic, or ellipsoidal). While QDA is much more expressive than LDA, it requires estimating $\mathcal{O}(K \cdot d^2)$ parameters, risking severe overfitting in high dimensions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Statistical decision theory}
\label{sec:statistical_learning_theory}

We now take a decisive forward step: transitioning from discrete probability models (Naïve Bayes) to \textbf{Statistical Learning Theory}, which provides the theoretical foundations for Deep Learning and Empirical Risk Minimization.

\subsection{Formal setup and notation}
\label{sec:slt_notation}

Let the learning framework be defined by the following mathematical entities:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Input Space} $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$: The domain of feature vectors $\mathbf{x}$.
  \item \textbf{Output Space} $\mathcal{Y}$: The domain of target labels (e.g., $\mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1\}$ for binary classification, $\mathcal{Y} = \{1, \dots, K\}$ for multi-class classification, or $\mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R}$ for continuous regression).
  \item \textbf{Data-Generating Distribution} $\mathcal{D}$: A fixed, unknown joint probability distribution $p(\mathbf{x}, y)$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$.
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis Class} $\mathcal{H}$: A family of candidate predictive functions $h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$.
  \item \textbf{Predictor / Model} $h \in \mathcal{H}$: A specific hypothesis parameterized by weights $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Task-dependent loss functions}
\label{sec:task_dependent_losses}

How do we measure the quality of a model's prediction? A model outputs a prediction $\hat{y} = h(\mathbf{x})$, while reality provides the true ground-truth target $y$. 

A \textbf{loss function} $\ell: \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \to \mathbb{R}^+$ quantifies the penalty or cost incurred by predicting $h(\mathbf{x})$ when the true target is $y$:
\begin{equation}
\ell(h(\mathbf{x}), y) \ge 0.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Core Machine Learning Principle: Loss selection depends on the task.}
A critical concept emphasized throughout the lectures is that \textbf{the mathematical formulation of the loss function strictly depends on the target task being solved.} Choosing a loss function that does not match the physical nature of the target output invalidates the optimization:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Continuous Regression Tasks} ($\mathcal{Y} = \mathbb{R}$): When targets are continuous physical quantities (e.g., house prices, temperatures, object coordinates):
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Mean Squared Error (MSE) / Quadratic Loss}:
    \begin{equation}
    \ell_{\text{MSE}}(h(\mathbf{x}), y) = (h(\mathbf{x}) - y)^2.
    \label{eq:mse_loss_def}
    \end{equation}
    Penalizes large errors quadratically; its expectation is minimized by the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathbf{x}]$.
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE) / $L_1$ Loss}:
    \begin{equation}
    \ell_{\text{MAE}}(h(\mathbf{x}), y) = |h(\mathbf{x}) - y|.
    \label{eq:mae_loss_def}
    \end{equation}
    Linear penalty; robust to extreme outliers; minimized by the conditional median.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Discrete Categorical Classification Tasks} ($\mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1\}$ or $\mathcal{Y} = \{1, \dots, K\}$):
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{The Ideal 0-1 Loss}:
    \begin{equation}
    \ell_{0-1}(h(\mathbf{x}), y) = \mathbb{I}(h(\mathbf{x}) \ne y) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } h(\mathbf{x}) = y, \\ 1 & \text{if } h(\mathbf{x}) \ne y. \end{cases}
    \label{eq:zero_one_loss_def}
    \end{equation}
    Directly measures classification error rate.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Entropy / Negative Log-Likelihood Loss}:
    When classifiers output a normalized probability distribution over $K$ classes $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}) \in [0, 1]^K$, the standard loss is Categorical Cross-Entropy:
    \begin{equation}
    \ell_{\text{CE}}(\hat{\mathbf{p}}, y) = -\sum_{k=1}^K y_k \log \hat{p}_k = -\log \hat{p}_y,
    \label{eq:cross_entropy_def}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{y}$ is the one-hot encoded ground-truth vector ($y_k = 1$ if class is $k$, else $0$).
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why surrogate continuous losses are mandatory.}
If our ultimate goal in classification is to minimize the 0-1 loss, why do we not optimize $\ell_{0-1}$ directly?
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item The 0-1 loss is a non-convex step function: its gradient with respect to model weights is \textbf{identically zero almost everywhere} ($\nabla_{\mathbf{w}} \ell_{0-1} = \mathbf{0}$), and undefined at the step threshold.
  \item A gradient of zero provides zero directional guidance for gradient descent: backpropagation cannot update weights.
  \item Minimizing 0-1 loss on a dataset is an NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem.
  \item Therefore, machine learning relies on \textbf{continuous, convex surrogate losses} (such as Cross-Entropy for neural networks, Logistic loss for logistic regression, and Hinge loss for Support Vector Machines) that provide smooth, informative non-zero gradients everywhere.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Risk and Bayes optimality}
\label{sec:true_risk_and_bayes_optimal}

The foundational objective of statistical learning theory is to evaluate and guarantee how a machine learning hypothesis $h \in \mathcal{H}$ will perform when deployed in the real world. To formalize this performance, we must distinguish between the performance on the observed training sample and the performance across the entire universe of data.

\paragraph{True Risk: The universal dataset evaluation.}
Let $\mathcal{D}$ denote the true, underlying joint probability distribution over the feature-label space $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$. The theoretical benchmark of performance is the \textbf{True Risk} (also known as \textbf{Expected Risk}, \textbf{Population Risk}, or \textbf{Generalization Risk}), denoted $R(h)$:
\begin{equation}
R(h) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim \mathcal{D}}[\ell(h(\mathbf{x}), y)] = \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}} \ell(h(\mathbf{x}), y) \, p(\mathbf{x}, y) \, d\mathbf{x} \, dy.
\label{eq:true_risk_definition}
\end{equation}
Crucially, $R(h)$ is evaluated over the theoretical \textbf{universal dataset}—an infinite population consisting of all possible input-output pairs that could ever be drawn from $\mathcal{D}$. 

Two key properties of True Risk must be kept in mind:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{$R(h)$ is model-dependent:} Notice that the hypothesis $h$ appears inside the expectation: $R(h)$ is a scorecard for a \emph{specific} model $h$. If we train a linear classifier $h_1$ and a deep neural network $h_2$, each possesses its own distinct True Risk: $R(h_1) \neq R(h_2)$. It quantifies the lifetime expected loss of that exact model across all future, unseen data.
  \item \textbf{$R(h)$ is fundamentally incomputable:} To evaluate the integral in \eqref{eq:true_risk_definition}, one must possess full analytical knowledge of the true joint data-generating distribution $p(\mathbf{x}, y)$. In practice, this distribution is unknown—if we already possessed $p(\mathbf{x}, y)$, machine learning would be entirely unnecessary because the optimal prediction could be calculated directly via Bayes' theorem.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The Bayes Optimal Classifier $h^*$.}
Given that every model has a True Risk $R(h)$, what is the absolute best conceivable predictor that could ever exist in the universe? 

The theoretical gold standard is the \textbf{Bayes Optimal Classifier} $h^*$, defined as the function that achieves the infimum of the True Risk over the space of \emph{all possible measurable functions} $\mathcal{F} = \{h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}\}$, unconstrained by any hypothesis family $\mathcal{H}$:
\begin{equation}
h^* \triangleq \argmin_{h \in \mathcal{F}} R(h).
\end{equation}
For classification under the standard $0$-$1$ loss $\ell_{0\text{-}1}(\hat{y}, y) = \mathbb{I}(\hat{y} \neq y)$, we can decompose the True Risk pointwise via the law of total expectation:
\begin{equation}
R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[ \mathbb{E}_{y \mid \mathbf{x}}[\ell_{0\text{-}1}(h(\mathbf{x}), y)] \right] = \int_{\mathcal{X}} \left( \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \mathbb{I}(h(\mathbf{x}) \neq y) \, P(Y = y \mid X = \mathbf{x}) \right) p(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}
Notice that for each individual feature vector $\mathbf{x}$, the inner sum is simply the probability of making an error:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \mathbb{I}(h(\mathbf{x}) \neq y) \, P(Y = y \mid X = \mathbf{x}) = 1 - P(Y = h(\mathbf{x}) \mid X = \mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}
Because $p(\mathbf{x}) \ge 0$, minimizing the overall integral $R(h)$ is equivalent to minimizing the inner error probability independently for each single coordinate $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}$. To minimize $1 - P(Y = h(\mathbf{x}) \mid X = \mathbf{x})$, we must maximize $P(Y = h(\mathbf{x}) \mid X = \mathbf{x})$. 

Therefore, the Bayes optimal decision rule assigns $\mathbf{x}$ to the class with the highest posterior probability:
\begin{equation}
h^*(\mathbf{x}) = \argmax_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(Y = y \mid X = \mathbf{x}).
\label{eq:bayes_optimal_classifier_pointwise}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Why we cannot achieve a zero error rate: The Bayes Risk $\mathcal{R}^*$.}
A natural question arises: \emph{If the Bayes Optimal Classifier has access to the exact true posterior distribution $P(Y \mid X)$ and possesses infinite functional capacity, why does it still fail to achieve an error rate of zero?}

The impossibility of achieving zero error stems from \textbf{irreducible aleatoric uncertainty} (intrinsic data ambiguity and overlapping class distributions). In real-world phenomena:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item The relationship between observed features $\mathbf{x}$ and the ground-truth target $y$ is almost never a deterministic mathematical bijection.
  \item Features $\mathbf{x}$ represent an incomplete, lossy measurement of the physical world. As a consequence, two distinct physical entities can have the \textbf{exact same observed feature vector $\mathbf{x}$} while possessing \textbf{different true labels $y$}.
  \item Because any classifier $h$ must output a single deterministic prediction $\hat{y} \in \mathcal{Y}$ for a given query $\mathbf{x}$, it cannot predict both classes simultaneously.
\end{itemize}

When the Bayes optimal classifier selects $h^*(\mathbf{x}) = \argmax_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(Y = y \mid X = \mathbf{x})$, its pointwise error rate on that specific input coordinate $\mathbf{x}$ is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(h^*(\mathbf{x}) \neq Y \mid X = \mathbf{x}) = 1 - \max_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(Y = y \mid X = \mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}
Unless the posterior distribution is strictly degenerate (i.e., $\max_{y} P(Y = y \mid X = \mathbf{x}) = 1$ almost everywhere, meaning complete determinism), this pointwise error is strictly positive.

Integrating this unavoidable pointwise ambiguity over the entire feature space yields the \textbf{Bayes Risk} $\mathcal{R}^*$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}^* \triangleq R(h^*) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[ 1 - \max_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(Y = y \mid X = \mathbf{x}) \right].
\label{eq:bayes_risk_formula}
\end{equation}
The Bayes Risk represents the \textbf{aleatoric floor of the universe}. No machine learning algorithm, no deep neural network with billions of parameters, and no quantum computer can ever achieve a True Risk lower than $\mathcal{R}^*$:
\begin{equation}
R(h) \ge \mathcal{R}^*, \quad \forall h \in \mathcal{F}.
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[Irreducible Aleatoric Error: Clinical Diagnosis and the Biased Coin]
\label{ex:bayes_risk_intuition}
To build an indelible mental picture of the distinction between True Risk and Bayes Risk, consider the following two canonical settings:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Clinical Diagnostic Screening (Overlapping Feature Profiles):}  
  Suppose two patients walk into a hospital and present with the \emph{identical} clinical measurement vector:
  \begin{equation*}
  \mathbf{x} = [\text{Age: } 50, \; \text{Resting BP: } 135/85, \; \text{Fasting Glucose: } 105, \; \text{Total Cholesterol: } 220].
  \end{equation*}
  Due to unobserved genetic variations and environmental factors not captured in $\mathbf{x}$, the true biological ground-truth distribution is:
  \begin{equation*}
  P(\text{Cardiovascular Disease} \mid \mathbf{x}) = 0.80, \qquad P(\text{Healthy} \mid \mathbf{x}) = 0.20.
  \end{equation*}
  An omniscient Bayes classifier $h^*$, possessing perfect knowledge of these probabilities, must choose a single diagnosis. It rationally predicts \textbf{Cardiovascular Disease}, because $0.80 > 0.20$.
  
  What happens across 100 patients presenting with this exact feature profile $\mathbf{x}$? 
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item 80 patients truly have the disease $\implies h^*$ is correct.
    \item 20 patients are healthy $\implies h^*$ misdiagnoses them!
  \end{itemize}
  Even with perfect omniscience, the classifier makes an unavoidable error on $20\%$ of these instances. That $20\%$ error is the \textbf{Bayes Risk} at $\mathbf{x}$. It cannot be eliminated without measuring new, orthogonal features (such as genetic sequencing) that separate the overlapping distributions.
  
  \item \textbf{The Biased Coin Game (Strategy vs. Intrinsic Randomness):}  
  Consider predicting the outcome of repeated coin flips where the coin is biased to land \textbf{Heads 70\% of the time} ($P(\text{Heads}) = 0.70$) and \textbf{Tails 30\% of the time} ($P(\text{Tails}) = 0.30$).
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \emph{The Bayes Optimal Predictor $h^*$:} The optimal strategy is to always predict Heads.
    \item \emph{The Bayes Risk $\mathcal{R}^*$:} Because Tails occurs $30\%$ of the time, even the optimal strategy suffers an error rate of $\mathcal{R}^* = 0.30$. No player can achieve an error rate below $30\%$.
    \item \emph{True Risk of Candidate Models $R(h)$:} 
    \begin{itemize}
      \item A random guessing model $h_{\text{random}}$ that predicts Heads with probability $0.5$ and Tails with probability $0.5$ achieves a True Risk of:
      \begin{equation*}
      R(h_{\text{random}}) = 0.5 \times 0.30 + 0.5 \times 0.70 = 0.50.
      \end{equation*}
      \item A superstitious model $h_{\text{pessimist}}$ that always predicts Tails achieves a True Risk of $R(h_{\text{pessimist}}) = 0.70$.
      \item An optimal learner $h_{\text{opt}}$ that converges to always predicting Heads achieves $R(h_{\text{opt}}) = 0.30 = \mathcal{R}^*$.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{intuition}[The Universal Risk Decomposition: Dissecting Generalization Error]
\label{box:risk_decomposition}
To understand how learning algorithms bridge the gap between empirical data and optimal performance, consider any hypothesis $h \in \mathcal{H}$ trained from a finite sample $\mathcal{S}_N$. Its total True Risk $R(h)$ can be decomposed into three fundamental, orthogonal components:
\begin{equation}
R(h) - \mathcal{R}^* = \underbrace{\left( \inf_{f \in \mathcal{H}} R(f) - \mathcal{R}^* \right)}_{\textbf{Approximation Error (Bias)}} + \underbrace{\left( R(h) - \inf_{f \in \mathcal{H}} R(f) \right)}_{\textbf{Estimation Error (Variance)}}.
\label{eq:excess_risk_decomposition}
\end{equation}
Adding the irreducible noise $\mathcal{R}^*$ gives the full hierarchy of risk:
\begin{equation}
R(h) = \underbrace{\mathcal{R}^*}_{\substack{\text{\textbf{Bayes Risk}} \\ \text{(Irreducible aleatoric noise)}}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{\text{approx}}(\mathcal{H})}_{\substack{\text{\textbf{Approximation Error}} \\ \text{(Hypothesis space capacity)}}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{\text{est}}(\mathcal{H}, N)}_{\substack{\text{\textbf{Estimation Error}} \\ \text{(Finite sample penalty)}}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{\text{opt}}}_{\substack{\text{\textbf{Optimization Error}} \\ \text{(Algorithm sub-optimality)}}}.
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Bayes Risk $\mathcal{R}^*$:} The floor set by nature. Cannot be reduced by collecting more data or expanding the model.
  \item \textbf{Approximation Error $\mathcal{E}_{\text{approx}}$:} The penalty for restricting our search to a specific model family $\mathcal{H}$ (e.g., linear models cannot fit a quadratic boundary). Decreases as the capacity of $\mathcal{H}$ increases.
  \item \textbf{Estimation Error $\mathcal{E}_{\text{est}}$:} The penalty for optimizing on a finite training set of size $N$ rather than the universal dataset. Increases with model complexity but decreases towards zero as sample size $N \to \infty$.
  \item \textbf{Optimization Error $\mathcal{E}_{\text{opt}}$:} The penalty if the numerical optimization algorithm (e.g., gradient descent) fails to find the empirical empirical risk minimizer due to non-convexity, saddle points, or early stopping.
\end{itemize}
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Empirical risk minimization}
\label{sec:erm_principle}

Because the true distribution $\mathcal{D}$ is unknown, machine learning operates in the \textbf{Batch Learning} regime:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item We are provided with a finite training dataset of $N$ instances drawn i.i.d. from $\mathcal{D}$:
  \begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}_N = \{(\mathbf{x}_1, y_1), (\mathbf{x}_2, y_2), \dots, (\mathbf{x}_N, y_N)\} \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{D}.
  \end{equation}
  \item By the \textbf{Law of Large Numbers}, the expected value over the true distribution can be approximated by the sample average over the observed training set.
\end{itemize}

This sample average is the \textbf{Empirical Risk} $\hat{R}_N(h)$:
\begin{equation}
\hat{R}_N(h) \triangleq \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ell(h(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i).
\label{eq:empirical_risk_definition}
\end{equation}
The foundational learning paradigm introduced by Vladimir Vapnik is the \textbf{Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM)} principle:
\begin{equation}
h_{\text{ERM}} \triangleq \argmin_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \hat{R}_N(h) = \argmin_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ell(h(\mathbf{x}_i), y_i).
\label{eq:erm_principle_equation}
\end{equation}
ERM replaces the impossible problem of minimizing True Risk $R(h)$ with the computationally feasible problem of minimizing the average loss on the observed training sample.

\subsection{PAC learning theory}
\label{sec:pac_learning_theory}

Can we guarantee that a hypothesis $h_{\text{ERM}}$ that achieves low empirical risk $\hat{R}_N(h)$ will also achieve low true risk $R(h)$ on unobserved test data?

Leslie Valiant's framework of \textbf{Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) Learning} establishes the mathematical conditions under which empirical learning is guaranteed to succeed~\cite{shalev2014understanding}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Approximately Correct}: We cannot demand that an algorithm finds a hypothesis with exactly zero error ($R(h) = 0$). Instead, we demand that the true generalization error is bounded by an accuracy tolerance parameter $\epsilon > 0$:
  \begin{equation}
  R(h) - R(h^*) \le \epsilon.
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Probably Correct}: Because our training dataset $\mathcal{S}_N$ is drawn stochastically from $\mathcal{D}$, there is always a non-zero probability that the random sample happens to be completely unrepresentative (e.g., drawing 100 straight coin flips of heads from a fair coin). We cannot guarantee success with $100\%$ certainty. Instead, we demand that the learning algorithm succeeds with high probability:
  \begin{equation}
  P_{\mathcal{S}_N \sim \mathcal{D}^N}\Big( R(h) - R(h^*) \le \epsilon \Big) \ge 1 - \delta,
  \label{eq:pac_guarantee}
  \end{equation}
  where $\delta \in (0, 1)$ is the failure confidence parameter.
\end{itemize}

\begin{theorem}[PAC Sample Complexity for Finite Hypothesis Spaces~\cite{shalev2014understanding}]
Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a finite hypothesis space. For any distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and loss bounded in $[0, 1]$, to guarantee that with probability at least $1 - \delta$, the ERM hypothesis satisfies $R(h_{\text{ERM}}) \le \min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} R(h) + \epsilon$, it suffices to draw a training sample size $N$ satisfying:
\begin{equation}
N \ge \frac{1}{\epsilon} \left( \ln |\mathcal{H}| + \ln \frac{1}{\delta} \right).
\label{eq:pac_sample_complexity}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
Equation~\eqref{eq:pac_sample_complexity} reveals the fundamental price of model capacity: as the size of the hypothesis space $|\mathcal{H}|$ grows, the sample complexity $N$ required to guarantee generalization increases logarithmically.

\subsection{Asymmetric loss matrices}
\label{sec:asymmetric_cost_matrix}

In Section~\ref{sec:true_risk_and_bayes_optimal}, we derived the Bayes Optimal Classifier under the symmetric $0$-$1$ loss function, proving that the risk-minimizing rule assigns $\mathbf{x}$ to the class with highest posterior probability ($P(Y=1 \mid \mathbf{x}) \gtrless 0.5$). 

However, in real-world engineering, clinical, and safety-critical domains, \textbf{the costs of different misclassification errors are fundamentally asymmetric}~\cite{bishop2024deep}:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \emph{Medical Oncology}: Classifying a malignant cancer as benign (\textbf{False Negative}) delays life-saving chemotherapy, typically leading to patient death. In contrast, classifying a benign lesion as malignant (\textbf{False Positive}) results in an unnecessary secondary biopsy—anxiety-inducing and costly, but rarely fatal. Missing cancer is vastly worse than a false alarm.
  \item \emph{Autonomous Vehicles}: Classifying an empty shadow as a pedestrian causes an uncomfortable braking event. Classifying a real pedestrian as an empty shadow causes fatal collision.
  \item \emph{Cybersecurity / Fraud Detection}: Flagging a legitimate transaction as fraudulent causes minor customer inconvenience; missing a multi-million-dollar cyberattack causes catastrophic systemic insolvency.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The General Loss Matrix Formulation.}
To formalize asymmetric costs mathematically, we introduce a \textbf{loss matrix} $\mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$ where entry $L_{k, j}$ defines the exact penalty incurred for predicting class $\mathcal{C}_j$ when the ground-truth state of nature is class $\mathcal{C}_k$:
\begin{equation}
L_{k, j} \triangleq \operatorname{Loss}\left( \text{predict } \mathcal{C}_j \;\middle|\; \text{true } \mathcal{C}_k \right) \ge 0.
\label{eq:loss_matrix_def}
\end{equation}
Typically, correct classifications incur zero loss ($L_{k, k} = 0$), while incorrect classifications carry strictly positive penalties ($L_{k, j} > 0$ for $j \ne k$).

When presented with an observed feature vector $\mathbf{x}$, the system does not know the true class with absolute certainty, but possesses the posterior distribution $P(\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x})$. The \textbf{expected conditional loss (conditional risk)} of selecting decision $\mathcal{C}_j$ is the expectation over all possible true classes:
\begin{equation}
R(j \mid \mathbf{x}) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x}}\left[ L_{k, j} \right] = \sum_{k=1}^K L_{k, j} P(\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x}).
\label{eq:conditional_risk_general}
\end{equation}
By the fundamental principle of statistical decision theory, the optimal decision rule partitions the input space into decision regions $\mathcal{R}_1, \dots, \mathcal{R}_K$ such that each coordinate $\mathbf{x}$ is assigned to the class that minimizes expected risk:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}(\mathbf{x}) = \argmin_{j \in \{1, \dots, K\}} \sum_{k=1}^K L_{k, j} P(\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x}).
\label{eq:optimal_decision_rule_loss_matrix}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Analytical Derivation of the Optimal Threshold for Binary Classification.}
Consider binary classification with classes $\mathcal{C}_1$ (e.g., disease present) and $\mathcal{C}_2$ (disease absent). The loss matrix is:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{L} = \begin{pmatrix} L_{1, 1} & L_{1, 2} \\ L_{2, 1} & L_{2, 2} \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $L_{1, 2}$ is the cost of a False Negative and $L_{2, 1}$ is the cost of a False Positive.

The decision rule assigns $\mathbf{x}$ to class $\mathcal{C}_1$ if and only if the expected risk of choosing $\mathcal{C}_1$ is strictly less than choosing $\mathcal{C}_2$:
\begin{equation}
R(1 \mid \mathbf{x}) < R(2 \mid \mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}
Expanding using Equation~\eqref{eq:conditional_risk_general}:
\begin{equation}
L_{1, 1} P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) + L_{2, 1} P(\mathcal{C}_2 \mid \mathbf{x}) < L_{1, 2} P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) + L_{2, 2} P(\mathcal{C}_2 \mid \mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}
Grouping identical posterior probabilities:
\begin{equation}
(L_{1, 2} - L_{1, 1}) P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) > (L_{2, 1} - L_{2, 2}) P(\mathcal{C}_2 \mid \mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}
Since $P(\mathcal{C}_2 \mid \mathbf{x}) = 1 - P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
(L_{1, 2} - L_{1, 1}) P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) > (L_{2, 1} - L_{2, 2})\Big( 1 - P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) \Big).
\end{equation}
Expanding and collecting $P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x})$ on the left-hand side:
\begin{equation}
\Big[ (L_{1, 2} - L_{1, 1}) + (L_{2, 1} - L_{2, 2}) \Big] P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) > L_{2, 1} - L_{2, 2}.
\end{equation}
Assuming that misclassifications are strictly more costly than correct classifications (so the bracketed term is strictly positive), dividing yields the exact optimal decision threshold:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{ P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) > \frac{L_{2, 1} - L_{2, 2}}{(L_{1, 2} - L_{1, 1}) + (L_{2, 1} - L_{2, 2})} }
\label{eq:asymmetric_decision_threshold}
\end{equation}
When correct decisions incur zero penalty ($L_{1, 1} = L_{2, 2} = 0$), this fundamental equation simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) > \frac{L_{2, 1}}{L_{1, 2} + L_{2, 1}} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{L_{1, 2}}{L_{2, 1}}}.
\label{eq:simplified_asymmetric_threshold}
\end{equation}

\begin{examinsight}[Numerical Walkthrough: The Cancer Diagnosis Threshold Shift]
In oral examinations, students are often asked: \emph{``If missing a cancer diagnosis is 100 times more severe than a false alarm, at what posterior probability should we classify a patient as positive?''}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Let False Negative cost $L_{1, 2} = 100$ and False Positive cost $L_{2, 1} = 1$.
  \item Correct diagnosis costs $L_{1, 1} = L_{2, 2} = 0$.
  \item Substituting into Equation~\eqref{eq:simplified_asymmetric_threshold}:
  \begin{equation}
  P(\text{Cancer} \mid \mathbf{x}) > \frac{1}{100 + 1} = \frac{1}{101} \approx 0.0099 \quad (0.99\%).
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
The optimal decision threshold does not reside at $0.5$; it drops to \textbf{less than $1\%$}! If the model estimates just a $1\%$ chance of malignancy, the risk-minimizing rational action is to classify the patient as positive and immediately order follow-up diagnostic procedures.
\end{examinsight}

\subsection{The reject option}
\label{sec:reject_option}

In many automated decision systems, forced binary or categorical classification is inappropriate when the model is uncertain. For regions of the feature space where class posteriors are nearly balanced ($P(\mathcal{C}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}) \approx P(\mathcal{C}_2 \mid \mathbf{x}) \approx 0.5$), any automated decision incurs a substantial probability of error.

Statistical decision theory resolves this through the \textbf{Reject Option} (or \emph{Doubt Option})~\cite{bishop2024deep}: permitting the model to abstain from making an autonomous decision, instead routing the instance to a human specialist (e.g., radiologist or senior fraud investigator).

Let $\theta \in [0, 1]$ be the fixed cost of choosing the reject action (e.g., the labor cost of human review).
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item If an automated decision is made, the expected misclassification loss is $1 - \max_k P(\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x})$.
  \item If the system abstains, it incurs the fixed rejection penalty $\theta$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore, the system chooses to execute an automated prediction if and only if the error risk is strictly less than the cost of rejection:
\begin{equation}
1 - \max_{k} P(\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x}) < \theta \iff \max_{k} P(\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x}) \ge 1 - \theta.
\label{eq:reject_option_condition}
\end{equation}
If $\max_k P(\mathcal{C}_k \mid \mathbf{x}) < 1 - \theta$, the instance falls into the \textbf{Rejection Region}, triggering an alert for human expert intervention. Figure~\ref{fig:asymmetric_costs_and_reject_option} illustrates both asymmetric threshold shifting and the reject option band.

\subsection{Decision theory for regression}
\label{sec:regression_decision_theory}

How does statistical decision theory operate when target outputs are continuous real numbers ($y \in \mathbb{R}$)? 

Let $h(\mathbf{x})$ be our regression predictor, and let $\ell(h(\mathbf{x}), y)$ be a continuous loss function. The expected True Risk is:
\begin{equation}
R(h) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \ell(h(\mathbf{x}), y) \, p(\mathbf{x}, y) \, dy \, d\mathbf{x} = \int_{\mathcal{X}} \left[ \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \ell(h(\mathbf{x}), y) \, p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy \right] p(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}
Because $p(\mathbf{x}) \ge 0$, minimizing overall risk $R(h)$ is achieved by independently minimizing the inner conditional expectation for each single coordinate $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}$:
\begin{equation}
h^*(\mathbf{x}) = \argmin_{a \in \mathbb{R}} \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \ell(a, y) \, p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{1. The Squared Error Loss: Optimal Predictor is Conditional Expectation.}
Let the loss be quadratic: $\ell(h(\mathbf{x}), y) = (h(\mathbf{x}) - y)^2$.
Using the calculus of variations, we differentiate the inner integral with respect to $h(\mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial h(\mathbf{x})} \int_{\mathcal{Y}} (h(\mathbf{x}) - y)^2 p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy = 2 \int_{\mathcal{Y}} (h(\mathbf{x}) - y) p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy = 0.
\end{equation}
Distributing the integral:
\begin{equation}
h(\mathbf{x}) \underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{Y}} p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy}_{= 1} - \int_{\mathcal{Y}} y \, p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy = 0.
\end{equation}
Solving for $h(\mathbf{x})$ yields the fundamental theorem of regression:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{ h^*(\mathbf{x}) = \int_{\mathcal{Y}} y \, p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathbf{x}] }
\label{eq:optimal_regression_conditional_expectation}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Under squared error loss, the theoretically optimal predictor is the conditional expectation (conditional mean) of the target given the features!}

\paragraph{Exact Decomposition into Reducible Loss and Irreducible Bayes Noise.}
To analyze why a model cannot achieve zero regression risk, we decompose $(h(\mathbf{x}) - y)^2$ around the optimal predictor $\mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}]$~\cite{bishop2024deep}:
\begin{align}
(h(\mathbf{x}) - y)^2 &= \Big( \big( h(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] \big) + \big( \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] - y \big) \Big)^2 \nonumber \\
&= \big( h(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] \big)^2 + \big( \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] - y \big)^2 + 2 \big( h(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] \big) \big( \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] - y \big).
\end{align}
Now, take the conditional expectation over $y \mid \mathbf{x}$. Notice what happens to the cross-term:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{y \mid \mathbf{x}}\left[ 2 \big( h(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] \big) \big( \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] - y \big) \right] = 2 \big( h(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] \big) \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y \mid \mathbf{x}}\left[ \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] - y \right]}_{= \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] - \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] = 0} = 0.
\end{equation}
The cross-term vanishes identically! Substituting back into the full True Risk:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{ R(h) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} \big( h(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] \big)^2 p(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x} + \int_{\mathcal{X}} \operatorname{Var}(Y \mid \mathbf{x}) p(\mathbf{x}) \, d\mathbf{x} }
\label{eq:regression_risk_decomposition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:regression_risk_decomposition} is a celebrated result in machine learning:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{First Term (Reducible Model Error)}: Depends on our choice of hypothesis $h(\mathbf{x})$. It is non-negative and reaches its minimum of zero if and only if our model perfectly learns the true conditional expectation $h(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}]$.
  \item \textbf{Second Term (Irreducible Bayes Noise)}: Represents the intrinsic variance $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid \mathbf{x})$ of the data generation process. It is completely independent of the model $h(\mathbf{x})$. No matter how much compute, data, or parameters an algorithm possesses, expected test error can never fall below this irreducible noise floor.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{2. The Absolute Error ($L_1$) Loss: Optimal Predictor is Conditional Median.}
What if we choose the $L_1$ loss $\ell(h(\mathbf{x}), y) = |h(\mathbf{x}) - y|$?
Differentiating with respect to $h(\mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial h(\mathbf{x})} \int_{\mathcal{Y}} |h(\mathbf{x}) - y| p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy = \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \operatorname{sign}(h(\mathbf{x}) - y) p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy = 0.
\end{equation}
Splitting the integral into regions where $y < h(\mathbf{x})$ and $y > h(\mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^{h(\mathbf{x})} p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy - \int_{h(\mathbf{x})}^\infty p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \, dy = 0 \implies P(Y \le h^*(\mathbf{x}) \mid \mathbf{x}) = P(Y \ge h^*(\mathbf{x}) \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}
\textbf{Under $L_1$ absolute loss, the optimal predictor is the conditional median $\operatorname{median}(Y \mid \mathbf{x})$!} The median is inherently robust to extreme outlier perturbations, making $L_1$ loss the principled choice for datasets with heavy-tailed sensor anomalies.

\subsection{Losses from probability models}
\label{sec:parametric_loss_construction}

In Section~\ref{sec:task_dependent_losses}, we observed that loss functions depend on the target task. A profound unifying principle established in modern deep learning literature~\cite{prince2023understanding,goodfellow2016deep} is that \textbf{loss functions are not heuristic inventions; they are derived directly as the Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) of parametric probability distributions}:
\begin{equation}
\ell(\hat{y}, y) \triangleq -\ln p(y \mid \phi(\mathbf{x})),
\label{eq:loss_as_nll_recipe}
\end{equation}
where $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ represents the parameters of a probability distribution predicted by the machine learning model.

\paragraph{1. Gaussian Distribution Yields Mean Squared Error (MSE).}
Suppose the continuous target $y \in \mathbb{R}$ is perturbed by homoscedastic Gaussian noise:
\begin{equation}
p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(y; \mu(\mathbf{x}), \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(y - \mu(\mathbf{x}))^2}{2\sigma^2} \right).
\end{equation}
Let our model predict the mean: $\mu(\mathbf{x}) = h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$. The negative log-likelihood of a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \ln p(y_i \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i))^2 + \frac{N}{2} \ln(2\pi \sigma^2).
\end{equation}
Assuming constant variance $\sigma^2$, the second term is constant and $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}$ is a positive scaling factor. Minimizing NLL is \textbf{identically equivalent to minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE)}:
\begin{equation}
\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \equiv \argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i) - y_i)^2.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Heteroscedastic Regression: Predicting Uncertainty.}
In real-world data, noise is rarely uniform (homoscedastic); some input regions have high measurement ambiguity while others are precise. Prince~\cite{prince2023understanding} emphasizes that neural networks can predict \textbf{both} the mean $\mu(\mathbf{x})$ and the input-dependent variance $\sigma^2(\mathbf{x})$ (heteroscedastic regression) using a dual-head architecture:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{hetero}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ \frac{(y_i - \mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i))^2}{\sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i)} + \ln \sigma^2_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i) \right].
\label{eq:heteroscedastic_nll}
\end{equation}
The network automatically learns to predict high variance $\sigma^2(\mathbf{x})$ for noisy, ambiguous inputs to avoid massive squared-error penalties, while the second log-variance term prevents the trivial collapse $\sigma^2 \to \infty$.

\paragraph{2. Laplace Distribution Yields Mean Absolute Error (MAE / $L_1$).}
Suppose target deviations follow a Laplace distribution:
\begin{equation}
p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{Laplace}(y; \mu(\mathbf{x}), b) = \frac{1}{2b} \exp\left( -\frac{|y - \mu(\mathbf{x})|}{b} \right).
\end{equation}
The negative log-likelihood is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{b} \sum_{i=1}^N |h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_i) - y_i| + N \ln(2b).
\end{equation}
Minimizing this NLL is \textbf{identically equivalent to minimizing Mean Absolute Error (MAE / $L_1$ loss)}.

\paragraph{3. Bernoulli Distribution Yields Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE).}
For binary classification $y \in \{0, 1\}$, let the model predict the Bernoulli success probability $\hat{p} = \sigma(z(\mathbf{x})) \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
p(y \mid \mathbf{x}) = \hat{p}^y (1 - \hat{p})^{1 - y}.
\end{equation}
The negative log-likelihood is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \Big[ y_i \ln \hat{p}_i + (1 - y_i) \ln(1 - \hat{p}_i) \Big],
\end{equation}
which is the celebrated \textbf{Binary Cross-Entropy Loss}.

\paragraph{4. Categorical Distribution Yields Multiclass Cross-Entropy.}
For multi-class classification $y \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, the output follows a Categorical distribution parameterized by softmax probabilities $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}) \in [0, 1]^K$:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{k=1}^K \hat{p}_k^{y_k}.
\end{equation}
The negative log-likelihood evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k=1}^K y_{ik} \ln \hat{p}_{ik} = -\sum_{i=1}^N \ln \hat{p}_{i, y_i},
\end{equation}
which is the standard \textbf{Categorical Cross-Entropy Loss} ubiquitous in deep learning.

\subsection{Maximum likelihood and KL divergence}
\label{sec:mle_kl_equivalence}

We can now establish the profound unification connecting statistical estimation to information theory~\cite{goodfellow2016deep,bishop2024deep}:
\begin{theorem}[Equivalence of Maximum Likelihood and KL Divergence Minimization]
Let $\tilde{p}_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}, y) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \delta(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i, y - y_i)$ be the empirical data distribution defined by a training sample $\mathcal{D}$. Maximizing the sample log-likelihood under a parametric model $p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}, y; \boldsymbol{\theta})$ is mathematically identical to minimizing the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the empirical data distribution and the model distribution:
\begin{equation}
\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \sum_{i=1}^N \ln p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \equiv \argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} D_{\text{KL}}\Big( \tilde{p}_{\text{data}} \;\Big\|\; p_{\text{model}}(\cdot; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \Big).
\label{eq:mle_kl_equivalence}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Expanding the definition of KL divergence in Equation~\eqref{eq:kl_divergence_def}:
\begin{align}
D_{\text{KL}}\Big( \tilde{p}_{\text{data}} \;\Big\|\; p_{\text{model}} \Big) &= \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim \tilde{p}_{\text{data}}}\left[ \ln \frac{\tilde{p}_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}, y)}{p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}, y; \boldsymbol{\theta})} \right] \nonumber \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim \tilde{p}_{\text{data}}}\left[ \ln \tilde{p}_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}, y) \right] - \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim \tilde{p}_{\text{data}}}\left[ \ln p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}, y; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right].
\end{align}
Notice that the first term $\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{p}_{\text{data}}}[\ln \tilde{p}_{\text{data}}]$ is the negative empirical entropy of the dataset, which depends strictly on the fixed training data and is completely independent of the model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. 

Evaluating the expectation in the second term over the empirical distribution $\tilde{p}_{\text{data}}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y) \sim \tilde{p}_{\text{data}}}\left[ \ln p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}, y; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ln p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}).
\end{equation}
Therefore:
\begin{equation}
\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} D_{\text{KL}}\Big( \tilde{p}_{\text{data}} \;\Big\|\; p_{\text{model}} \Big) = \argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left( -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \ln p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \right) = \argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \sum_{i=1}^N \ln p_{\text{model}}(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}).
\end{equation}
\end{proof}
This fundamental equivalence proves that Maximum Likelihood Estimation is not an arbitrary optimization heuristic; it is the unique statistical procedure that minimizes information-theoretic divergence from reality to model.

\subsection{Point estimator bias and Bessel's correction}
\label{sec:point_estimators_and_bessel}

To complete our foundational statistical grounding, we formalize the properties of \textbf{point estimators}~\cite{goodfellow2016deep,bishop2024deep}. 

A \textbf{point estimator} $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_N = g(\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N)$ is a mathematical function mapping an observed sample of $N$ data points to an estimate of the true underlying population parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$. Because the training sample is stochastically drawn from $\mathcal{D}$, the estimator $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_N$ is itself a \textbf{random variable} possessing its own sampling distribution, mean, and variance.

\paragraph{Estimator Bias and Standard Error.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Estimator Bias}: The difference between the expected value of the estimator across infinite hypothetical samples and the true parameter:
  \begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_N) \triangleq \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}^N}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_N] - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*.
  \label{eq:estimator_bias_def}
  \end{equation}
  An estimator is \textbf{unbiased} if $\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_N) = \mathbf{0}$, meaning $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_N] = \boldsymbol{\theta}^*$. It is \textbf{asymptotically unbiased} if $\lim_{N \to \infty} \operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_N) = \mathbf{0}$.
  \item \textbf{Estimator Variance and Standard Error}: The variance of the estimator across sample realizations is $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_N)$. The \textbf{Standard Error (SE)} is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution. For the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i$ of i.i.d. variables with variance $\sigma^2$:
  \begin{equation}
  \operatorname{SE}(\bar{x}) = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\left( \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i \right)} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{i=1}^N \sigma^2} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}.
  \label{eq:standard_error_formula}
  \end{equation}
  This establishes the classic $\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})$ rate of statistical convergence.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Analytical Proof of Bias in Sample Variance and Bessel's Correction.}
Consider drawing $N$ independent observations $x_1, \dots, x_N \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N x_i$ is an unbiased estimator of $\mu$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[x_i] = \mu.
\end{equation}
The Maximum Likelihood Estimator for the variance evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ML}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2.
\label{eq:mle_sample_variance}
\end{equation}
What is the expected value of $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ML}}$? Does it equal the true variance $\sigma^2$?

\begin{proof}[Proof of Bias in Sample Variance~\cite{bishop2024deep}]
Expand the deviation $x_i - \bar{x}$ by centering around the true population mean $\mu$:
\begin{equation}
x_i - \bar{x} = (x_i - \mu) - (\bar{x} - \mu).
\end{equation}
Squaring both sides:
\begin{equation}
(x_i - \bar{x})^2 = (x_i - \mu)^2 - 2(x_i - \mu)(\bar{x} - \mu) + (\bar{x} - \mu)^2.
\end{equation}
Summing over all $N$ data points:
\begin{align}
\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 &= \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2 - 2 (\bar{x} - \mu) \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)}_{= N(\bar{x} - \mu)} + N (\bar{x} - \mu)^2 \nonumber \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2 - 2 N (\bar{x} - \mu)^2 + N (\bar{x} - \mu)^2 \nonumber \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \mu)^2 - N (\bar{x} - \mu)^2.
\label{eq:variance_algebraic_identity}
\end{align}
Now, take the mathematical expectation of Equation~\eqref{eq:variance_algebraic_identity}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \right] = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}\left[ (x_i - \mu)^2 \right] - N \mathbb{E}\left[ (\bar{x} - \mu)^2 \right].
\end{equation}
Notice the two expectations:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item By definition of population variance: $\mathbb{E}[(x_i - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$, so the sum evaluates to $N \sigma^2$.
  \item By definition of the variance of the sample mean: $\mathbb{E}[(\bar{x} - \mu)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}$.
\end{enumerate}
Substituting these two expectations:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \right] = N \sigma^2 - N \left( \frac{\sigma^2}{N} \right) = N \sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (N - 1) \sigma^2.
\end{equation}
Dividing by $N$ to evaluate the expectation of the Maximum Likelihood estimator:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{ \mathbb{E}[\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ML}}] = \frac{N - 1}{N} \sigma^2 }
\label{eq:mle_variance_bias_result}
\end{equation}
\end{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:mle_variance_bias_result} is a foundational theorem: \textbf{the Maximum Likelihood estimator of variance is systematically biased downward by a factor of $\frac{N-1}{N}$!} 

Because the sample mean $\bar{x}$ is calculated from the exact same data points, the deviations $(x_i - \bar{x})$ are measured relative to the empirical sample center rather than the true center $\mu$. The sample points are mathematically closer to their own average $\bar{x}$ than to the true mean $\mu$, systematically underestimating the spread.

To construct an \textbf{unbiased estimator of variance}, we multiply by $\frac{N}{N-1}$, yielding the celebrated \textbf{Bessel's Correction}:
\begin{equation}
S^2 \triangleq \frac{1}{N - 1} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \implies \mathbb{E}[S^2] = \sigma^2.
\label{eq:bessels_correction_unbiased}
\end{equation}
The denominator $N - 1$ reflects the loss of one \textbf{degree of freedom}: once the sample mean $\bar{x}$ is computed, only $N - 1$ deviations are freely variable because $\sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$.

\paragraph{The Bridge to Chapter~\ref{sec:optimization_and_gradient_descent}: From Probabilistic Risk to Iterative Optimization.}
With the complete foundations of probability theory, information theory, generative modeling, statistical decision theory, and empirical risk minimization established, a fundamental computational challenge emerges.

In classical generative models (Naïve Bayes, LDA), parameter estimation admits closed-form analytical formulas (counting frequencies or inverting sample covariance matrices). However, when we transition to discriminative models parameterized by non-linear compositions (such as Logistic Regression and Deep Neural Networks), setting the empirical risk gradient to zero $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \hat{R}_N(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{0}$ yields transcendental, non-linear algebraic systems where closed-form solutions do not exist.

To navigate continuous loss landscapes, minimize empirical risk, and optimize non-linear classifiers, machine learning must abandon closed-form algebraic inversions in favor of first-order numerical optimization: \textbf{Gradient Descent}. How to formulate loss geometries, escape saddle points, and navigate non-convex surfaces forms the foundational core of Chapter~\ref{sec:optimization_and_gradient_descent}.
